# Chapter 21 — The Mirror Court

*The court in which every witness shared one source*

The Mirror Court had no corrupt judges.

That was its corruption.

The Mirror Court’s great promise was that no judge would be imposed from above. Every claim would be tested by the same standards, and the standards themselves would be evaluated by the court.

Its first case concerned a school that had expelled a boy for refusing a ritual gesture. The school cited the covenant. The boy cited consent. The court cited its own definitions of covenant and consent, all written by members of the court.

Mara asked who had authority to challenge those definitions, and the chief judge named a procedure created by the court, interpreted by the court, and appealable to a chamber whose members the same body had appointed.

He stood and asked the boy what the gesture had cost him.

The answer did not fit the court’s categories. The boy said the ritual made his body feel occupied by other people’s certainty. He did not claim the rite was false. He claimed he could not perform it honestly.

The judges translated the statement into conscientious objection and began debating exceptions.

“Stop,” Mara said. “You have converted his testimony before allowing it to answer you.”

The mirrors trembled.

For one breath, every reflection showed an empty courtroom.

The judges adjourned. The boy remained outside the procedure, but his unconverted words were entered into the record. That small breach later became the first door through which the court could be overruled by the person bearing its cost.

A later case exposed the same closure under a different name.

When Alis, the woman who remembered an unwitnessed son, petitioned the court, she was asked whether her claim was empirical, phenomenological, operational, metaphysical, or theological.

“He was my child,” she said.

“That is not a register.”

“Then your registers are missing a room.”

The court recorded her answer as resistance to classification.

Eiren sat among the judges and felt the architecture closing around them. The court was not ignoring Alis. It was attending to her with exquisite precision while converting everything she said into evidence of the court’s need.

The mirrors along the walls reflected judges, clerks, and witnesses into an apparent multitude. From the center of the room, one could mistake repetition for independence.

Mara stood in the gallery.

“Who judges the judge?” she asked.

The chief justice pointed to the appellate bench.

“Who trained them?”

“The House.”

“Who defines the grounds of appeal?”

“The House.”

“Who decides whether a ground exists outside those definitions?”

Silence.

Eiren ordered every mirror covered.

Alis was allowed to state her claim without translation. It remained unverified. It also remained hers. The court could not make it public fact, but it could protect her from being called mad merely because the event had left only one witness.

Eiren had a blank space carved beneath the court’s governing inscription. No sentence filled it. The emptiness acknowledged truths for which the House possessed no honest tribunal.

The judges found the blank unsettling.

He counted that among its functions.

One judge did not adjourn.

Her name was Corran, and she had sat on the bench for nineteen years, and she stayed in her seat while the others filed out and the clerks began taking down the mirrors.

“You have not fixed it,” she said.

Eiren waited.

“You have covered the mirrors and carved a hole in the wall and told yourself that the hole is humility. In four years the hole will have a keeper. In seven it will have a form to be filled in before you are permitted to approach it. In twelve there will be a man whose whole office is the administration of the things your House admits it cannot judge, and he will be a good man, and he will be the most powerful man in this town, and you will not see it happen because you will be looking at the hole and feeling honest.”

“Then tell me what to build instead.”

“I do not know. That is not the same as being wrong.”

She gathered her papers and went out past the covered glass.

He entered her objection in the record because the discipline required it, and he expected, for some years, to be able to answer it.

He never did. Neither did Neris. The blank space beneath the inscription acquired a keeper in the twelfth year, and the keeper was a good man, and by then Corran was dead and there was no one left who had said it first.
