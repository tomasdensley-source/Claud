# Chapter 3 — The Calling of Names

*The old rite of active memory*

Every household in Verrin kept a bowl for names. The wealthy had bowls of silver or blue glass. The poor used wood. Eiren’s family bowl was plain clay, repaired along one side with a dark metal staple so old that no one remembered who had driven it.

At the midyear Calling, the family also set a little witness-pot beside every living person. Early in the season, Eiren’s youngest sister held a folded paper above his and asked, “Why do I write for people who can hear me?”

“Because hearing is not the same as being witnessed,” their father said. “Write one true thing only you noticed. Then another life will be carrying it.”

Through the season, relatives slipped such papers into the pots: one true memory, one precise kindness, one fault that had been forgiven without being renamed, one ordinary act that proved a life had occupied more than a chair. The pots guarded against the flattering blur by which a person could be praised into disappearance.

Uncle Rian arrived after the roots had been served, carrying Eiren’s youngest sister on one shoulder and a wooden bird on the other. The bird had belonged to her for three years and possessed only one wing. Rian had given it a second wing carved from pear wood, too large and slightly crooked, so the toy looked permanently surprised.

“It will never fly straight,” the girl complained.

“Neither do geese,” Rian said. “They still arrive.” He set the bird on the table and made it hop between the bowls until Grandmother Asha threatened to put him in the stew.

Rian was Eiren’s father’s younger brother, a man who repaired small things badly enough that everyone could see where love had entered the work. He sang one line ahead of the hymns, remembered the wrong endings to jokes, and never crossed the threshold without touching the lintel twice. Earlier that week he had promised Eiren that they would return to the old field and copy the carvings beneath the throne before the waterworks buried them.

“You read the first line,” he had said. “I will read the one the stone has forgotten. Between us we may invent something almost accurate.”

His witness-pot carried a blue line around the rim because Rian claimed all plain clay looked accused of something.

The Calling began with the dead. Eiren’s sister drew the name of a grandmother who had died before she was born.

“She had gray hair,” the girl said.

“That is description,” their father replied. “Tell what only a life could have done.”

The girl looked helplessly around the table. Assistance was permitted only after failure had been allowed to become honest.

At last she said, “She cut the burned part from the bread and ate it herself.”

Their father nodded. “Then she is remembered.”

The old religion trusted such sentences more than grand praise. At funerals, one witness spoke of kindness, another of anger, another of a promise kept, another of a harm never repaired. A polished saint was considered dangerously close to a forgotten person. Accuracy gave the dead more shape than reverence alone.

Rian drew next. The paper named Grandmother Asha, who sat opposite him and objected before he had opened his mouth.

“Do not tell the story about the goat.”

“The goat is your clearest theological contribution.”

“The goat was stolen.”

“Borrowed by an animal with initiative.”

The children laughed. Rian told instead how Asha had walked six miles in winter to return a measure of flour she believed had been counted twice. Asha corrected the distance to four and the weather from snow to hard rain. Rian accepted both corrections with the gravity of a priest receiving revelation.

“Then she is remembered accurately enough to remain dangerous,” he said.

The Calling was not usually solemn. Children complained when the bowl came out. Old people corrected one another’s dates. Someone always remembered a kindness another person insisted had been vanity. The rite survived because it was woven into supper rather than separated from life behind a temple door.

After the dead came the living. Each person opened one folded paper from the little pot beside the bowl. Eiren’s mother read that she hummed while cutting pears. His father read that he checked every latch twice and denied doing so. Eiren’s sister read that she lied when frightened and confessed before the lie had time to become furniture.

Then their father reached toward the blue-lined pot.

It was empty.

He turned it over. Nothing fell out.

For a moment every person at Grandmother Asha’s table looked toward the chair beside it.

The chair was empty too.

A cup stood there with a thumbprint in the milk. A spoon rested across the bowl. The wooden bird sat near the edge of the table, its new pear-wood wing bright against the older body. The seat cushion was pressed in the middle as though someone had risen only a breath before.

No one said Rian’s name.

Eiren’s father frowned at the pot. “Why did we set the salt jar here?”

“That is not the salt jar,” Eiren said.

His mother moved it beside the stove. “It is now.”

His father gathered the cup and spoon. “Someone set an extra place,” he said, with an anger the small mistake did not explain.

The children resumed the Calling because children trust adults to tell them when terror has entered a room. The count came out correctly. Six people had eaten. Six chairs were needed. The cup became an extra cup. The spoon had been laid out by mistake. The toy bird, according to Eiren’s sister, had been bought at the spring market.

Grandmother Asha did not resume.

She touched the blue line with one finger and began crying so quietly that at first Eiren thought the lamplight was moving on her face.

“Why are you crying?” he asked.

She looked around the table as though happiness had become too large to bear.

“I’m not sure,” she said. “I just feel happy to be with you all.”

No one found the answer strange. His mother kissed Asha’s temple. His father poured more milk. The family remained warm, ordinary, and complete.

Eiren reached beneath the blue-lined pot and found a folded paper pressed flat against the table. It carried no name and no memory. He slipped it into his pocket before his mother turned the pot into a salt jar forever.

Later, after everyone slept, he carried the blank paper to the window. The old field lay beyond the roofs, almost invisible except where moonlight caught the remaining knees. He whispered the names spoken that night. It was cool as a river stone by the last name.

There was someone missing from the list.

The knowledge had no face, no date, and no evidence. It was not even grief. It was the shape grief might leave if a person had been removed and the wound instructed not to remember what caused it.

Eiren wrote in his small book: A blank is not always an absence of information. Sometimes it is the only surviving information about an absence.

He stared at the sentence until the letters began to exchange places. He crossed them slowly, hand over hand, and copied the line again. The second copy was clearer.

In the morning the blue coat hanging beside the door belonged to no one. His mother said a traveler must have left it. Grandmother Asha folded it carefully and placed it beneath her bed.

Sister Ilya arrived carrying the parish ledger in both arms. The volume contained births, deaths, covenants, separations, adoptions, reconciliations, and names the town had promised never to reduce to numbers. Eiren found the page corresponding to their household.

There was a gap between his father and his youngest sister.

Not an empty line. The ink itself bent around a space where a line should have been.

Sister Ilya touched it, then withdrew her finger.

“Paper settles,” she said.

“Paper does not carve a second wing.”

She looked at the wooden bird. “Your sister bought that at market.”

“No. Someone made it last night.”

“Who?”

The question opened inside him and found no name.

His mother made a warding sign. Sister Ilya’s eyes sharpened.

“Have you sat in the seat?”

The question entered the room before its meaning did.

Eiren lied.

“No.”

Sister Ilya closed the ledger. “The rite is not a game, Eiren. We remember actively because passive memory rots. A name sleeping in a book is not the same as a name carried by a living mind.”

“Why?”

“Because what has no witness returns to the unmade.”

“That is a prayer.”

“It is also a warning.”

She took the blank paper. For one instant, before it disappeared into her sleeve, Eiren saw a letter upon it.

It was the first letter of his own name.
