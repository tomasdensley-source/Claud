# Chapter 2 — A City Made Gentle

*A city in the season of pears*

Verrin had never been rich, but neither had it learned the ordinary cruelties by which poor places are disciplined.

Morning in Verrin began slowly. Bakers opened after sunrise because no body needed food urgently enough to justify waking in darkness. The market sold more fruit than grain, more repaired tools than new ones, and more stories than either. A person might work three hard hours and spend the afternoon mending a roof, visiting an aunt, or doing nothing beside the stream without risking starvation.

The peace of the animals entered the people’s habits. Dogs did not guard houses so much as announce guests. Deer sometimes crossed the lower gardens while children watched from the walls. The northern bears were large, but they moved away from human scent and had never entered a home in living memory. Hunters still carried spears, because skill without consequence becomes pageantry, but few expected the woods to hate them.

Verrin possessed tools older than any workshop capable of making them, beams of dark steel that had not rusted in a thousand rains, glass that bent without breaking, and sealed devices whose uses had become ceremonial or comic. Yet the town had little appetite for grand invention. Why build engines to conquer distance when distance seldom killed? Why force ten harvests from a field that already gave three more than the people required?

The waterworks proposal was therefore not a desperate measure. It was civic ambition, the kind that gave councilors a reason to speak about the future and masons a reason to order new stone.

Councilor Odran displayed the plans in the market hall. He was a patient man with square hands and a habit of listening before he disagreed. The new channels would catch spring runoff, settle the silt, and carry cleaner water to the lower streets. Children would not have to climb to the old cistern. Dyers would stop fouling the east stream. No one opposed clean water.

The argument concerned the field.

“There are other fields,” said Sister Ilya, keeper of the town’s names.

“None as level,” Odran replied. “None beside the old conduit.”

“It is consecrated ground.”

“It was.”

That small tense entered the hall like a draft.

Eiren stood near the back with Mara and Torren. He had spent three days telling himself the sixth turn had always been there. The house beside it belonged to an old woman called Ven, according to everyone he asked. Mara remembered stealing pears from her tree. Torren claimed Ven had once struck him with a broom. Eiren could remember neither theft nor broom nor old woman.

When he returned to the road alone, the house remained. The pear tree had a scar where a branch had been cut years earlier. Inside, a clock struck a quarter no one had asked about, and Ven stood with two cups in her hands before returning one to the shelf. Reality had not made a hurried correction. It had provided history.

“What is wrong with you?” Mara asked after the council meeting.

“Nothing.”

“You have looked over your shoulder seven times.”

“Six.”

She smiled, then saw that he was serious. “What are you counting?”

He almost told her.

Then Torren imitated Sister Ilya’s solemn voice: “It was consecrated ground,” and the others laughed. Eiren imagined explaining the road, imagined their faces searching his for the joke, imagined becoming the next old warning nobody understood.

“Steps,” he said.

Verrin did not need the new system to survive. It wanted easier washing, fewer hours carrying jars uphill, and an accomplishment to point to. Odran’s plan was reasonable enough to make the old field seem unreasonable by comparison.

After the meeting, Sister Ilya caught Eiren by the sleeve.

“You counted the road again,” she said.

He looked at her sharply.

“Your lips move.”

“There are six turns.”

“There are five.”

“Come with me.”

She did. They walked the southern road twice. On the first passage, Ilya counted five. Eiren counted six. On the second, she paused before the pear tree and stared at the house beside it.

“Ven has lived there since before your mother was born,” she said.

“Do you remember visiting her?”

Ilya opened her mouth. No memory arrived. Her face did not become frightened. It became formal, as though fear had been asked to wait outside until a ritual could be found.

“Do not speak of this in the market,” she said.

“Why?”

“Because a truth may be real before it is safe to carry in a crowd.”

That night, his mother prepared the meal with less grain than usual. Not because they lacked it, but because the body in Verrin did not require much to be content. A bowl of roots, a little bread, pears preserved in honey, and warm milk fed six people. His youngest sister fell asleep before the names were called.

Their father woke her gently.

“No one sleeps through remembrance,” he said.

They joined hands around the table.

Outside, beyond the last houses, wind moved across the field and lifted dust from the broken god’s knees.
