# Chapter 6 — The One More Turn

*He did not yet have another*

The sixth turn began appearing elsewhere.

Eiren began testing the sixth turn at different hours. At dawn it bent around Ven’s house. At noon the road sometimes passed straight through the space where the garden should have been, though anyone walking with him remembered only the ordinary route. At dusk, the pear tree cast two shadows, one toward the west and one toward the old field.

He tied thread between the cedar and the baker’s wall. The next morning the thread remained, but its knots had moved. He drove a small nail into the road’s edge. By evening the nail was embedded in the threshold of Ven’s house, polished by generations of feet. When he pried it out, his own initials were worn into the head.

Mara became his second witness reluctantly. She did not see every change, but she believed that his counting was not performance. That distinction mattered more than agreement.

“What do you want me to do?” she asked.

“Remember that I said it.”

“That is all?”

“For now.”

“Then say it exactly.”

They stood beside the split cedar. Eiren described the five-turn road of his childhood, the new bend, the house, and the portrait with the missing man. Mara repeated the account back to him without improving it. She added what she had seen and marked what she had not.

It was their first deliberate act of active memory, though neither knew the term as anything more than a supper rite.

Torren found them and mistook precision for conspiracy.

“You have built a little church of two,” he said.

“No,” Mara replied. “We are keeping our roads separate.”

The answer irritated him because it denied both submission and rebellion. He wanted every person either inside the inherited order or gloriously outside it. A witness who remained uncertain offered no stage.

The irregularity spread beyond the southern road.

A stair in the House of Formation acquired an extra landing. A corridor behind the dye market lengthened after dusk. Mara entered a pantry and emerged from the chapel vestry with flour on her sleeves and no memory of crossing the street.

The town had names for such things. A road could be crossed by a curse. A house could become thin. The Wall could misdirect a traveler who approached it without reverence. Older people accepted these explanations with the calm of those who had inherited a world furnished with mystery.

The young compared notes.

Comparison was new.

Their grandparents had encountered an impossible turn and told a keeper. Torren encountered one and drew a map. Mara measured the distance between two doors. Bern and Loris, the dye-house brothers, marked a repeating alley with yellow thread so they could prove the repetition to one another.

The thread disappeared overnight.

“Someone removed it,” Torren said.

“Who?”

“The keepers.”

“Why would they care about an alley?” Eiren asked.

Torren’s answer came too quickly. “Because every warning is a fence, and every fence has an owner.”

Torren had invented nothing supernatural. He had merely found the pleasure of treating suspicion as intelligence and contempt as freedom.

Mara was less certain. “Some fences keep animals out. Some keep people in. You have to look at the ground on both sides.”

They went together to the extra road.

The old woman’s house stood where Eiren remembered emptiness. Ven welcomed them, gave them tea, and told stories of Mara stealing pears. Mara laughed before each ending. Torren remembered the broom. Eiren listened for a seam and found none.

On the wall hung a family portrait. Ven stood beside a man whose face had been cut away from the paper. The cut was old. The remaining figures leaned subtly inward, as though the missing person had occupied more space than the gap allowed.

“Who was he?” Eiren asked.

Ven followed his gaze.

“Who?”

He pointed.

Her expression emptied, then filled with irritation. “There is no one there. Paper tears.”

When they left, Mara whispered, “I saw him.”

“You saw the hole.”

“No. I saw a hand on her shoulder. For a moment.”

Torren had seen nothing. He was angry that they had.

That evening the town bells rang the wrong number of times. Most people did not notice. Those who did blamed the bell keeper. The bell keeper insisted he had rung correctly.

Eiren opened his small book and wrote:

*The world does not become strange when the curse begins. The curse begins when a person becomes able to see that it was strange already.*

He crossed out the word curse.
