# Prologue — Before the First Room

*The first distinction*

When Eiren was nine, Sister Ilya showed the children the page no copyist was permitted to finish.

The old book lay open beneath a glass weight in the House of Formation. Gold leaf crowded its margins. Rivers became branches, branches became veins, and every vein returned toward a circle at the center. The circle itself had been left bare. Time had yellowed the parchment around it, but the emptiness remained strangely pale, as though no dust had ever been allowed to settle there.

Torren leaned over the rail. “They ran out of gold.”

“They had gold,” Ilya said.

“Then they ran out of ideas.”

Mara covered a smile with her sleeve. Eiren looked at the unpainted center and felt the small unease of a door found standing open in an empty house.

Ilya reached toward the bare circle. Her finger stopped above it as if the page were hot. When she drew her hand back, she folded it into the other.

Ilya began the oldest lesson.

“Before there was light,” she said, “there was a difference.”

The children knew the answer and gave it together. “One thing stood where another did not.”

“And from difference?”

“Relation.”

“And from relation?”

“The Record.”

Verrin still trained children to carry the sentences even when their parents had ceased expecting the sentences to carry anything back.

Ilya placed a black seed on the left side of the page and a white seed on the right. “What changed when I set down the second?”

“There were two seeds,” said Mara.

“There was comparison,” said Eiren.

“There was a mess,” said Torren.

“All three,” Ilya replied. “And now that we have seen the mess?”

“We clean it.”

“Perhaps. But seeing has changed what walking away means. That change is the Glaw.”

The word sounded old even then. It did not mean punishment, though adults often used it as though the world kept a rod behind its back. It meant that knowledge entered the knower into what followed. Before the crack beneath a bridge was seen, the traveler was merely crossing. Afterward, each uncarried clamp became part of the account.

Ilya moved the seeds closer to the empty circle.

“No mind can hold the whole Record at once,” she said. “A life must be allowed to remain a life. Terror must arrive as warning before it arrives as machinery. Distance must appear as a road. The unbearable must wear a form that can be approached.”

“The Wall,” the children answered.

“The Wall,” Ilya said. “A mercy, when it gives truth by measure.”

“And a prison when the keepers want it to be,” Torren said.

Ilya did not rebuke him. “Every mercy may be enthroned. A wall can protect a room or prevent anyone inside from learning there is a door. Rest can restore a body or make neglect feel gentle. A story can carry truth or perfume delay. That is why the Wall is called unreliable.”

“Because it breaks?” Eiren asked.

“Because it refuses to become a perfect map. A perfect map of the depths would be another doorway into them.”

She turned the page. The next image showed a seated figure surrounded by pillars. His face had once been painted, but weather or a careful knife had removed it. Beneath the throne ran lines like roots, canals, or writing too small for human eyes.

“Who is he?” Mara asked.

“One of the First Ones.”

“A god?” said Torren.

“A man?” said Eiren.

Ilya looked at the faceless figure. “The old books do not require those answers to quarrel.”

She led them to the final lines. The children stood, because the rite said one should not speak them seated.

“What has no witness,” Ilya began.

“Returns to the unmade.”

“The god may depart.”

“The covenant remains operative.”

Torren spoke the words in the deep ceremonial voice that always made Mara laugh. Eiren did not laugh. His attention had returned to the bare circle.

“What belongs there?” he asked.

“Nothing we can draw without making it smaller.”

“Then why leave it?”

“So no answer may sit down and mistake itself for the center.”

Years later, Eiren would build a House around that refusal. He would give rooms to questions, roads to evidence, dates to errors, and an exit to descendants. He would also learn that an open center could become another ornament if nobody was willing to cross what opened there.

On that morning, he knew only that the page was old, Torren was bored, Mara was trying not to smile, and Sister Ilya’s hand trembled when she closed the book.

Almost none of them believed the words referred to anything.
