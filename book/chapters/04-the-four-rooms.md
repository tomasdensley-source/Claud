# Chapter 4 — The Four Rooms

The old House of Formation stood at the center of Verrin, though it was not one house and had never been built at one time.

Its first room was a dining hall. There the children learned kinship, loyalty, and the names of those who had carried them before they could carry themselves. They were taught that a person who forgets the table will mistake loneliness for freedom.

Its second room was a chapel of black stone. Lamps burned before an empty arch. There the children learned promises, discipline, and the hope that a life could be joined to something larger than appetite. They were taught that a clean promise can carry a person when feeling cannot.

Its third room opened directly onto the road. Satchels hung beside the door. Travelers taught the children how to knock, how to speak to strangers, how to keep walking after refusal, and how to offer help without owning the threshold.

Its fourth room was a study crowded with letters.

Here Eiren’s war was private. The marks did not stay still for him. They crowded, traded places, and leaned into one another until a sentence became a rope bridge in winter. He crossed slowly, hand over hand, while the other children ran.

The teachers praised speed. Eiren learned another kind of reading: deliberate, effortful, exact. He also learned to hide how much it cost.

Each room gave him a garment. The dining hall gave a heavy coat. The chapel gave a white sash. The road gave boots. The study gave gloves stained with ink. The garments were good, but no one had measured whether they fit together.

The ceremony of readiness was held in public, and public ceremonies have a way of turning discomfort into a test of character. Eiren’s father fastened the heavy family coat. Sister Ilya tied the white sash. A traveler buckled the road boots. The master of letters pulled the inked gloves over Eiren’s hands and praised the neatness of the fit.

Each garment represented a gift he genuinely valued. The coat carried generations who had fed him before he knew hunger. The sash carried promises that had outlived appetite. The boots carried the courage to cross another person’s threshold without demanding ownership. The gloves carried the discipline through which a difficult mind could still reach exactness.

Together the garments almost stopped his breathing. From the crowd Mara saw his shoulders rise with each shallow breath. She began to step forward, then stopped when the elders applauded. To interrupt would have made his discomfort visible. To remain silent allowed the ceremony to call the discomfort virtue.

Nara was the House mender, keeper of torn hems, split seams, and garments made for bodies the makers had not imagined. Everyone borrowed her scissors and no one returned them. She watched from the back with a third pair hanging from her belt.

Eiren walked the prescribed circle. The coat caught beneath the sash. The boots struck the stone heavily. “Look how well they fit him,” someone called from the crowd as his fingers went numb.

That night he returned to the empty central court and wrote in chalk upon the floor:

*I have been given many pieces, but I have not yet been given to myself.*

Mara found him there. Nara came behind her, carrying the scissors from her belt.

“I should have said something,” she told him.

“I should have refused.”

Nara looked from one to the other. “A child does not become responsible for an adult’s design merely because the child endured it politely. And a witness is not absolved merely because speaking would have been awkward. Both of you have work. Neither of you is the failure.”

She cut the first stitch.

“Say it now, while it costs an evening. Leave it unsaid, and one day the whole seam will split.”

The sound of the thread parting was almost nothing. Eiren felt air reach a place beneath his collar that had been pressed closed all day.

Nara did not erase the chalk sentence. She drew a square around it.

“Build here,” she said.

“Build what?”

“A fifth room. No throne. A worktable. Bring every inherited thing there before you let it command you.”

Eiren thought of the throne in the field, of Torren spreading his arms where the god’s arms had been, of his own brief weight in the seat.

“What is the difference between altering an inheritance and insulting it?”

Nara’s scissors paused.

“Cost,” she said. “The person who alters must bear the repair. The person who mocks leaves the cost for someone else.”

She gave him two sentences to keep.

The first was: *These rooms formed you. They are not entitled to finish you.*

The second was: *The mind drops stitches. Build a place that holds the deciding, or the rooms will decide again on your behalf and call it memory.*

Eiren copied both into a small book.

On the next page he drew a road with six turns.
