# Chapter 10 — The Two Springs

*The meeting rivers*

A merchant produced six letters praising his scales. All six had been written by customers who owed him money. When two healers claimed the same herb cured fever, Senn sent Eiren to discover whether both had learned from the same grandmother. When three priests dreamed of a black door, he asked whether they had slept under the same hymn.

“Agreement is not independence,” he said. “A river does not become two because it passes around an island.”

The Trial of the Meeting Rivers required paths to remain separate until their sources were known. Only then could convergence earn weight.

Eiren loved the discipline because it seemed capable of keeping the world still. If every claim had a home, every witness a road, every correction a date, perhaps reality could be defended from the sixth turn.

Senn taught through journeys.

Alis did not behave like a person asking to be believed. She behaved like a person who had spent too long being treated as evidence against herself. She kept her hands folded and answered only what was asked. When Senn repeated a question, she did not become more persuasive. She became tired.

Eiren examined the ashes. They contained traces of painted wood and a small curved nail such as toy makers used for wheels. None of it proved a son. A toy might have belonged to a niece, a neighbor, or a child imagined after years of longing.

“What would belief require of us?” Eiren asked.

“Nothing,” Alis said. “I did not come for a verdict. I came because if I die with the only memory, then whatever happened will have no witness at all.”

Senn’s face changed. He had been treating the account as a claim seeking promotion. Alis was seeking preservation.

They created a new jar. The label did not say Alis had a son. It said: Alis reports a son whose name she cannot retain. No independent witness has been found. Ashes from a burned painted object are preserved with the report.

Alis read the last line aloud. “The claim is singular and must not be mocked, promoted, or destroyed.”

“You left room for me to be wrong.”

“Yes,” Senn said.

“And room for him to have lived.”

“Yes.”

She wept then, not because the House had confirmed her, but because uncertainty had ceased being used as permission to erase her grief.

Weeks later Eiren passed Alis in the market. For a moment a boy walked beside her. He had ash on his hands and a wooden horse beneath one arm.

Alis was alone.
