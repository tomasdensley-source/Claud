# Chapter 29 — The Narrow Bridge

*The narrow bridge*

The Calix had been a bridge that failed the people it could not carry. Its admission records were preserved without ceremony, which made them more terrible.

The admission appeal belonged to a family named Rook. The parents had been accepted. Their eleven-year-old daughter had not. The technical language described her sensory and social cognition as incompatible with the stabilization model. The father’s reply contained one sentence: A lifeboat that asks us to leave our child is another form of the water.

The parents refused passage.

Tavian Rusk’s name appeared beneath the Rook decision and six other exclusions. Beside three he had written temporary; beside one, expand after crossing. The pressure of his pen had scarred the paper, but no later hand had reopened the cases.

Autistic people were among those excluded. So were others whose cognitive architecture differed in ways the builders could not model.

Other families chose differently. Some crossed and spent centuries defending necessity. Some crossed and never forgave themselves. Some remained beside excluded spouses, children, or siblings and died in the outer world. The Chronicle preserved none of them as simple heroes or cowards.

Eiren read the records slowly. His own difficulties with letters had often been treated as a private defect the world graciously accommodated. Here he saw a system built around a narrow expectation of how a mind should filter sensation, read social agreement, and accept symbolic mediation. The bridge had not failed because excluded people lacked humanity. It had failed because the engineers had not known how to carry the full range of humanity.

“And yet new minds like these were born inside,” Neris said.

The Chronicle showed children in later generations whose attention did not smooth the world in the expected way. They noticed repeating textures, inconsistent distances, and social contradictions others passed over; some were called cursed, some were hidden, and some became keepers because exact difference made them valuable. Native birth differed from transfer, so the Calix could generate minds it had once been unable to import, but its shelter remained designed around the original filter and poorly prepared to protect them.

Mara placed her hand against the record of the Rook family.

“Repair cannot mean returning to the first design,” she said.

Eiren crouched beside her. The record’s cold light crossed both their hands.

“No,” Eiren replied. “It must mean preserving the people while changing the shelter that fails them.”

Eiren understood his slow war with letters differently. It was not proof that he belonged to an excluded category, nor a simple key to revelation. It was one place where the inherited assumptions of the world had failed to fit the mind living inside it.
