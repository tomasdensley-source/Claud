# Chapter 17 — The Protest That Walked

*Do not build toward me. Build an exit.*

Mara was nineteen when the House attempted to define her.

The entry described her as a corrective witness, relational counterweight, and guardian of the Other’s irreducibility. It was respectful, precise, and intolerable.

She read it twice.

“You have turned my refusal to be assigned a role into a role.”

Eiren began explaining the purpose of the category.

She walked out.

The House had procedures for disagreement. It had no procedure for a person who refused to remain present while being interpreted.

Mara crossed Verrin, passed the market, and continued along the north road. Eiren followed at a distance until she stopped beside the stream.

“Do you want me to leave you alone?” he asked.

“I want you to answer a question without writing the answer inside your own architecture first.”

“Ask.”

“Can I be wrong about you without becoming a shadow figure?”

He said nothing.

“Can I misunderstand the House without becoming evidence of the House’s need?”

Eiren looked down at his empty hands. “I could write—”

“No.”

The stream moved between them.

“Can I leave without my departure being narrated as your lesson?”

She stepped onto the first stone in the water.

“Can I love you and refuse the role your love gives me?”

Eiren thought of the Other as a philosophical category and hated himself for it.

“Yes,” he said.

“That was too fast.”

“Then I do not know. But I want the answer to be yes, and I will build toward it.”

“Do not build toward me. Build an exit.” He returned to the One House and removed her entry from the live register. He did not move it to the Cold Hall. He placed it in the Evidence Cellar with a note:

An account of another person is not the person. The subject retains the right to refuse the role assigned by the account.

Then he unhinged a door in the eastern wall.

Mara did not return for three days.

The eastern door remained unhinged, and weather entered the House. Dust gathered in the corridor. A swallow flew through once and struck the Evidence Cellar window before finding its way out. Several keepers proposed rehanging the door until a better exit could be designed.

Eiren refused, though every draft felt like accusation.

When Mara returned, she did not congratulate him.

“An exit is not real because the architect permits it,” she said. “It is real when leaving does not convert the person into a criminal, a lesson, or a wound the House owns.”

“What else do I do?”

“You let me decide whether my account remains here.” The request frightened him. Her testimony had corrected some of the House’s deepest errors. Removing it would make the structure less complete.

Mara chose which records could remain, which required redaction, and which belonged to her alone. Eiren felt the loss in the archive as a physical gap. He also felt the relation between them become less useful and more real.

They wrote a new rule: No account of another person becomes inheritance merely because the recorder believes it is important. The Cold Hall preserved Mara’s objection under her own name and the date on which the House surrendered material it had wanted to keep.

The protest returned with terms.
