# Chapter 1 — The Throne Without a Face

*He was the only one who could tell*

There was a field beyond the last houses of Verrin where the grass grew too thick and where, now and then, a running foot struck something that did not give.

The field had once been a court, though nobody living could have described its boundaries. In spring the grass rose in long parallel bands where paving still resisted the roots. After hard rain, water gathered in shallow circles that were too regular to be ponds. Sheep would graze to the edge of those circles and turn aside without being driven. The people of Verrin treated these habits as quirks of soil and animal sense, because a place can remain strange for so long that strangeness becomes part of the weather.

Eiren had been uneasy in the field since childhood. He did not fear the remaining legs. They were too ruined to resemble a person. What troubled him was the throne between them. Its seat had survived every part of the god that might have used it. The empty chair seemed less like a remnant than an expectation.

At the center of the field a man sat who was not a man. He was stone, and vast, and most of him was gone. His head had left first, in a year no one had recorded. The shoulders went after it, then the arms, then the folded breadth of the torso. The hips had split long ago. Rain entered the cracks, winter widened them, and the stone came away grain by grain until the fallen god had become a low weather of pebbles around his own feet.

Only the legs remained clearly, great knees beneath a drapery carved for a body no longer there. Between them stood the throne.

The people knew the ruin had once been holy. They no longer knew what holiness had been for.

At the turning of the year they still repeated the old words. *The seat is not for sitting. What has no witness returns to the unmade.* They said them accurately and without expectation, the way a person might recite the dimensions of a house that had burned before their grandparents were born.

The council had voted to clear the field. Verrin needed a new place to settle and cleanse its water, and the old monument occupied good level ground. To practical people, a broken god who answered no prayer and grew no grain no longer outweighed clean water.

So five young people went out to laugh at it before it fell.

Mara noticed him looking.

“Do not tell me you still think he will come back for it,” she said.

“I don’t know who he was.”

“That has never stopped anyone from having an opinion about him.”

She kicked at a patch of turf and exposed a sliver of carved stone. The design was a chain of small open circles, each linked to the next. She covered it again with her heel, not reverently, but carefully.

Torren saw the gesture and laughed. “You two bow even when nobody is watching.”

“I was putting the dirt back,” Mara said.

“That is how bowing begins.”

Torren’s talent made caution feel ashamed of its own intelligence. When he climbed the steps, the others followed partly because they did not want him to own the afternoon. The first breach required neither demon, machine, nor command—only five young people who preferred being foolish together to appearing afraid alone.

They climbed the old steps. The throne was higher than it looked from the road. Grass grew from the joints. The seat sloped under centuries of rain, and its right arm had cracked away, leaving a rough shelf of stone.

Torren placed one foot on the lowest step.

“The seat is not for sitting,” Eiren said.

He meant it lightly. The warning left his mouth as a joke and arrived in the world as testimony.

Torren spread his arms. “Then it has waited a very long time for me.”

He climbed, turned, and sat where the First One had sat.

Nothing happened.

No light woke in the stone. No voice came from beneath the field. No door opened in the air. The grass moved in the grass’s way. A small white bird pecked at seed between the buried pillars and did not look up.

The five laughed, longer than the joke deserved, because relief often borrows the sound of contempt.

Mara sat beside Torren. Bern and Loris, the dye-house brothers, went up after him, Bern first, Loris steadying the broken arm with both hands so his brother could stand. Then Torren called Eiren a keeper’s pet, and Eiren, who feared ridicule more than the old god, climbed the steps and placed himself for one breath in the center of the empty seat.

The stone was warm.

That was all.

They returned to Verrin by the south road. Eiren counted the turns without deciding to. He had always counted when anxious: down the long slope, left at the well, right where the fence began, left beside the split cedar, then straight to the baker’s yard.

That evening there was another turn between the cedar and the baker’s yard.

He stopped in it.

The others continued ahead, arguing about whether the council would let them watch the demolition. None had noticed the new bend. It curved gently around a house Eiren did not remember, though the washing on its line had faded in the sun and a pear tree beside it had grown thick enough to shade the roof.

He counted again.

There were six turns where there had always been five.

He understood two things at once.

The road had changed.

He was the only one who could tell.
