# Chapter 16 — The Dye Market at Low Water

*A day that belonged to nobody else*

The dye market opened at the eighth bell and Mara had been at her table since the sixth, because the good light came off the water for about an hour before the stalls went up and after that it was gone for the day.

She was trying to hold a red.

The red she wanted lived in a beetle that fed on the pear trees on the north slope, and it lived there for about eleven days in the early summer, and if you took the beetles too early the colour came out the brown of a scab and if you took them too late it went to a thin pink that the first wash carried off down the drain. Her mother had known the eleven days by the smell of the orchard. Her mother had not written it down and had not been able to say it, and had once tried for a whole afternoon and had ended by pushing the pot across the table and saying, you will know it when the trees tell you.

The trees had not told Mara anything for four years.

So she had gone at it the other way, and the other way was ugly and slow and involved a great many pots. She had thirty of them on the table under a cloth, each with a scrap of undyed wool in it, each labelled with the day the beetles were taken and the temperature of the water when they went in and how long they had sat. The labels were on strips of birch bark because paper cost money and she was going through four labels a day.

Pela, who had once been lost behind a wall and had never since asked permission for anything, came down the row with a basket on her hip and lifted the cloth, which she was permitted to do and nobody else was.

“That one,” Pela said, and put a finger on the eleventh pot.

“That one is filthy.”

“That one is the colour of the inside of a mouth, and there is a woman in the upper town who will pay for the inside of a mouth and not tell her husband what she paid.” Pela set down the basket and took the wool out of the eleventh pot and held it against the back of her own hand, in the light, and turned her hand over. “You are trying to make the red your mother made.”

“I am trying to make a red that holds.”

“You are trying to make the red your mother made, and you are angry that it will not come, and you have thirty pots on this table because you would rather do arithmetic for a summer than sit in the orchard and admit that you cannot smell it.”

Mara took the wool back and put it in the pot and put the cloth over the pots.

They worked the rest of the morning without saying anything more about it, which was how the two of them had always worked, and at midday Pela went off with her basket and came back with bread and two pears and a hard yellow cheese, and they ate on the low wall with their feet hanging over the water, and the tide was out and the mud smelled the way the mud smelled, and a heron stood in the channel for a long time and then did not.

In the afternoon a dyer from the eastern villages came through the market pricing wool and stopped at the table.

He wanted to know what she was doing with thirty pots and she told him, and he listened all the way to the end without interrupting, which surprised her, and then he said that in his village they had lost the beetle red entirely, that the last woman who could take the beetles had died in the coughing winter, and that they now bought a red from the coast that came off on your hands.

“How long,” he said, “before you can say it in a sentence?”

“Two summers. Perhaps three.”

“And then you will sell it.”

“And then I will write it down and hang it on the wall of the market where any fool can read it, and in five years there will be a hundred people making it and I will be one of them and the price will be nothing, and my mother will still be dead, and the colour will still be here.”

The dyer thought about this. He was a heavy man, and the burn along his forearm marked him as a man who had held the ladle. Mara trusted him enough to wait.

“Why would you do that,” he said, “when you could keep it?”

“Because I have watched what happens to a thing when only one person is allowed to hold it, and I did not like any of it, and I have decided that I would rather be the woman who gave away a red than the woman who owned one.”

He bought nothing. He shook her hand, which dyers did not generally do, and went on down the row.

Mara worked until the light went off the water. Then she put the birch labels in her apron and carried the cloth home over her arm, and the mud smell followed her up the hill, and she thought about the eleventh pot the whole way, and about her mother pushing the pot across the table, and about the eleven days in the orchard that nobody had ever counted because everybody had simply known.

Somebody was going to have to count them.

She lit the lamp and got out the birch bark and began.
