# Chapter 40 — The Descendant’s Door

*One hand may refuse; one hand may receive*

Arda was Mara’s niece and the youngest keeper of the House.

Arda had grown up believing the One House was older than the town. Learning that Eiren had built it made the rooms feel less sacred and more dangerous. An ancient law could be endured as inheritance. A recent law had a person behind it, and persons could be questioned.

She spent her first week as chosen successor removing labels. The room called Singular Truth became simply a room with a table and a lock controlled by the person who brought the account. The Office of Compression lost its ceremonial colors. The eastern door received no replacement.

“You are stripping the memory from it,” one elder complained.

“No,” Arda said. “I am stripping ownership from the memory.”

Eiren watched her work while forgetting why he had chosen her. Each hour Mara reintroduced them.

“This is Arda,” she said. “She does not want your title. That is one reason you trusted her.”

Eiren smiled. “Then I was wise once.”

Arda asked him what must never be removed.

He considered the Four Beams, the four keepers, the five offices, the Glaw, the Cold Hall, the Wall, and the Hub. Every answer risked turning a tool into a command.

“Keep the right to answer back,” he said. “Everything else must earn its place again.”

“Including that sentence?”

“Especially that sentence.”

She wrote it on a scrap rather than carving it in stone.

When Eiren’s name vanished from his own mind, Arda did not tell him who he was as though identity could be restored by authority. She asked what he needed, and he answered from the remaining relation.

“A door that opens both ways,” he said.

He showed her the House.

“Some of this will feel like shelter,” he said. “Some will feel like walls. Keep what helps you tell the truth. Release what requires you to lie.”

“Am I allowed to dismantle it?”

“You must be.”

“Even after everything it cost?”

“Especially then. Cost does not purchase another person’s consent.”

Arda removed one door from its hinges and carried it outside.

The elders protested. Eiren smiled.

She opened a room no ancestor had named. It contained no shelf, symbol, register, or required question. It was kept available for knowledge that had not found a form and for grief that might never become material.

At the threshold, Arda held both hands visible.

One could refuse.

One could receive.

Arda asked Eiren, “Who taught you this?”

He opened his mouth.

His name was gone from him.

Arda sat in the empty fifth chair in the spring, during a council she had not been invited to.

Neris opened her mouth to tell her that the seat was not an office.

“I know what it is,” Arda said. “He left it empty so that you would all have to keep asking. You have stopped asking. I counted. Nobody has asked what changed in a word for two years and four months, and in that time you have revised the intake rite eleven times.”

The keepers looked at one another.

“So I will ask, and when you start asking again I will get up.”

She was not asked to leave. When the council ended, she got up.

The fifth chair remained empty.
