# Chapter 32 — The Cord-Cutter

*The mercy that can become abandonment*

The Cord-Cutter lived in a chamber where relationships appeared as threads of light.

The air smelled of hot copper and rain. Whenever one of the cords drew tight, it gave off a note too low for the ear and sharp enough to feel in the teeth.

The Cord-Cutter’s chamber did not present one moral answer. It presented cases.

A mother clung to an adult child whose departure would leave her alone. A husband called unlimited access proof of love. A keeper maintained a dangerous friendship because ending it felt like abandonment. A community expelled a difficult person and named the severance protection without asking whether less destructive boundaries had been possible.

Threads of light joined each person to others. Some cords nourished both sides. Some carried fear in one direction and obligation in the other. Some were so tight that neither person could move without injuring the other.

The Cord-Cutter offered Eiren a blade.

“Release is mercy,” it said.

“Sometimes,” Mara replied.

The chamber showed a thread between her and Eiren. Erasure had turned the connection into danger. Remembering him exposed her to the recursive truth. Cutting the cord would protect her and accelerate his disappearance.

“Do it,” Eiren said.

Mara took the blade and placed it on the floor.

“You do not get to turn my consent into your sacrifice.”

“It may save you.”

“Then ask. Do not make my safety the reason you own the decision.”

She chose to keep the connection for as long as she could bear it, with conditions: no technical explanation beyond what she had already seen, no demand that she remember through collapse, no claim that leaving would betray him.

Eiren asked Mara, “Do you want the thread kept?”

“Not at any cost. Not in any form. But yes, while I can answer.”

“You have not prevented loss,” it said.

“No,” Eiren replied. “We have prevented loss from impersonating choice.”

He turned to the thousands of other threads. Some needed protection. Some needed release. Some had become instruments of possession. The same action could be mercy in one relation and violence in another.

“You have answered well,” said the Cord-Cutter. “I am not persuaded.”

Eiren waited for it to explain itself, in the manner of every figure in the corridor, and it did not.

“You have made a good rule for the two of you, and the two of you are articulate, and you have hours in which to negotiate the terms of a cord, and you are both alive and both willing and both able to say what you want in a sentence. Now look at the ninth thread from the left.”

They looked.

“That is a woman in the lower town who has been kept alive for six years by a sister who will not survive another six. Ask her whether she wants the thread kept. She will say yes. She will say yes because she is frightened and because she loves her sister and because she has no other roof, and every word of it will be true, and her consent will be worth nothing, and in four years there will be two dead women instead of one, and your rule will have been followed perfectly at every step.”

“Then what do you want me to do?”

“Cut it. Now. Without asking her. She will hate you and she will live.”

“I will not do that.”

“I know,” said the Cord-Cutter, and it sounded tired. “You will go up and you will write your rule on the wall and it will be a good rule, and it will be quoted for a hundred years, and the ninth thread will burn out in four, and no one will connect the two things, because the rule will be right and the woman will merely be dead.”

The ninth thread belonged to Ista Verrow. Her sister was named Cael. Four years after the chamber showed them, both women died, four days apart, and Neris put their names on the same stone because there was no honest way to separate them.

Senn recorded the exchange. Under it, in the column reserved for resolution, he drew a line and left the space blank, and the space is still blank, and the House has never filled it.

The Cord-Cutter did not lower the blade. It offered it again.

Mara took the offered blade by its hilt. Before she could raise it, the edge widened into a threshold, the steel divided into upright posts, and a gate rose between them with one handle facing her and the other facing Eiren.

“Now it has two handles,” Mara said.

A wall decides what may be seen. A boundary decides what may be asked of me.

They altered the cord instead of worshiping it. Mara surrendered unlimited access to Eiren’s mind; Eiren stopped requiring her to carry every stage of his disappearance, and they established hours in which neither discussed erasure. They permitted distance without calling it betrayal. The cord became thinner and stronger.
