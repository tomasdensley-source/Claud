# Chapter 22 — The Demolition Field

*That does not make every stone a command.*

The work began before dawn.

Odran hoped to avoid a crowd. He brought masons, diggers, Hadrin, and the old survey machine. The plan was modest: expose the conduit, mark the foundation, and remove loose stone before the formal blessing of the waterworks.

The demolition machine was older than the council chamber. It moved on broad iron treads and carried a hammer whose jointed arm had once broken stone in places nobody remembered. The workers understood its levers through inherited practice rather than design. One lever raised. One turned. One made the old engine shudder awake with a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

People gathered before sunrise. Blue cords and white threads divided the crowd. Yellow cloth appeared everywhere without being organized.

Odran read the authorization. Sister Ilya read the prohibition. Torren climbed a wagon and read neither. He asked whether any people were free if a dead god could veto clean water.

Mara stood beside Eiren. “The question is fair.”

“He uses fair questions as hammers.”

“Then answer the question instead of complaining about the hand.”

The first shovel struck a pillar.

The second struck another.

By sunrise the field had become a geometry. Buried columns ran outward from the throne in rings and radial lines, far beyond the area anyone had imagined sacred. Verrin itself had been built partly over them. House foundations rested on ancient stone. Streets followed their arcs. The town had not grown beside the monument.

It had grown inside it.

The survey machine’s blue line touched one exposed carving and split into hundreds of threads. Each thread ran toward a house.

The workers stopped.

Odran did not.

“Old builders planned widely,” he said. “That does not make every stone a command.”

Hadrin knelt beside the conduit. Water moved beneath it with no visible source. The channel walls were made from the same dark steel found in ancient beams. Fine symbols pulsed along the surface.

Eiren recognized one.

It was the circular stain left by the blank token.

Torren climbed from the wagon to the throne itself. The crowd in yellow closed around its base. He announced that the keepers had hidden an entire city beneath the grass.

“They called roots what they knew were pillars,” he said.

This was false. It was also close enough to a true grievance to move people.

Someone threw a stone at Sister Ilya. Someone else cut the survey cords. A worker swung a hammer at the conduit.

Eiren stepped forward, but the machine struck before he reached the trench.

The hammer fell against the throne’s outer base.

No voice spoke. No light exploded. The stone opened with the ordinary sound of fracture.

Beneath it lay a hollow lined with black material and fine gold channels. The channels pulsed once, and every person in the field forgot why they had come.

For several breaths the crowd stood peacefully among machines, banners, and sacred ruin without a shared event to explain them. Then memory returned in different forms. Odran remembered authorizing repairs, not demolition. Ilya remembered a funeral. Torren believed the crowd had gathered to crown him in mockery. Eiren remembered only the sound of the strike.

The hammer stopped in the air.

Not against an obstacle. It simply ceased to descend.

The worker released it. The hammer remained suspended.

The crowd became quiet.

For one moment, every person in the field saw the missing god complete. He was tall and narrow, seated with his hands open. His face was human, tired, and terribly old. Around him the pillars rose unbroken. Beyond them stood a wall of light, not solid, but woven from countless moving marks.

Then the vision failed.

The hammer fell.

The machine’s second blow landed before anyone could agree it should stop.

Across Verrin, doors opened into the wrong rooms.
