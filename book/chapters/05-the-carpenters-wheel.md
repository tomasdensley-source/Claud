# Chapter 5 — The Carpenter’s Wheel

*The wheel without a final center*

Nara sent Eiren to the carpenter Hadrin, who made doors for houses that had settled crookedly and wheels for roads that refused to remain level.

Hadrin’s shop smelled of cedar, oil, and the mineral dust released by the old dark beams whenever one had to be cut. He disliked abstract discussions, but he would argue with wood for hours. “A board is allowed to contradict the carpenter,” he said. “That is how houses remain standing.”

The wheel stood upright in the center of the shop, taller than two men. Every spoke carried a question carved by a citizen of Verrin. Some were clean and formal. Others had been cut with kitchen knives and spelling errors. Hadrin refused to correct them. “The question belongs to the hand that cut it.”

Eiren added one of his own: How can a world change without anyone remembering the change?

Hadrin read it and made no joke.

He drew his plane once along a cedar board. The shaving curled between them.

“Does it happen often?”

“Often enough that I have begun writing down roads.”

“And do the roads obey the writing?”

“No.”

“Good.”

Eiren frowned.

“If the record commanded the world, you would have built a throne, not a witness.”

They turned the wheel together. The open center passed before Eiren’s eyes again and again. Through it he saw the shop, then Hadrin, then the door, then the evening road. The opening carried nothing, yet without it the wheel could not move.

“People will want you to fill it,” Hadrin said.

“They already do.”

“And you?”

Eiren thought of the field’s empty seat. “I want to know whether leaving it open is humility or fear.”

“Then do not carve humility there. Do the next honest thing and let the fruit answer later.”

The baker brought a question about hunger. The judge brought one about fairness. A widow brought one about death. A young couple asked whether love could survive truth. Sister Ilya asked whether the gods could hear after leaving the world. Mara asked why people praised obedience in children and agency in adults, as though one became the other without a wound between them.

Hadrin gave each question a spoke. When two seemed related, he added another between them. When a philosopher explained the whole wheel, the explanation required three more. Soon the wheel was so wide that no road in Verrin could hold it.

“The trouble is the hub,” the townspeople said. “Find the one answer in the center, and every spoke will know where it belongs.”

Hadrin tried duty, love, reason, survival, the covenant, and the will of the First Ones. Every center carried some spokes and distorted others. The wheel turned for a season, then cracked where the answer could not bear the load.

Eiren worked beside him for three years. He learned that wood answered honestly. A beam bowed where it was weak. A joint opened where the cut had lied. Craft did not care whether the carpenter’s explanation was beautiful.

One evening Hadrin removed the latest broken hub and stared into the opening.

“I have spent half my life discovering what belongs there,” he said.

Nara, who had come to collect a repaired chair, placed her good hand on the rim.

“A wheel is not made to worship its center,” she said. “It is made to carry weight across ground.”

“Then what fills the opening?”

“Some openings are measured. Some are crossed. Some remain open so you do not mistake your answer for the world.”

Hadrin set his chisel flat against the bench. “How did you know there was another turn?”

“I counted.”

“Everyone counts.” Hadrin rolled a shaving of cedar between his thumb and finger.

Eiren put one fingertip on the nearest carved word. “No. Everyone reads. Reading is a thing that happens to you. I have never had it happen to me. When the letters slide, the only way through is to put a finger on each one and hold it still, and then move the finger, and then hold the next one still. I have done that to every sentence I have ever crossed. So when the wheel turned, my finger was already down.”

Hadrin turned the wheel a quarter turn and watched the questions pass.

“Then you did not see more than us,” he said. “You were only slower in the one place where slowness was the instrument.”

Hadrin carved a sentence into the rim where the spokes ended: *Here proof reaches its shore.*

That night Eiren dreamed of the throne. The missing torso had returned, but the figure had no face. Behind it rose a wall made of falling letters. The letters never struck the ground. They vanished before impact, and every vanished letter took a small piece of the field with it.

He woke with the blank token beneath his pillow.

He had watched Sister Ilya take it away.

Now the first letter of his name had deepened on its surface.
