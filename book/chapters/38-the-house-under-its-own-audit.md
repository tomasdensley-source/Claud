# Chapter 38 — The House Under Its Own Audit

*The archive before its own tribunal*

Before the Wall could be repaired, the One House had to surrender its claim to innocence.

The audit was public, and that decision nearly destroyed the House before it saved it.

People arrived with harms the keepers had considered minor. A widow said her grief had been preserved so thoroughly that strangers knew its architecture better than she did. A carpenter said the House’s forms had required him to translate bodily dread into acceptable language before anyone would close an unsafe bridge. Mara opened the old entry that had made her a role inside Eiren’s system. “Relational counterweight,” she read aloud.

The room went silent.

Eiren listened without answering intent.

When a witness said the House had consumed evenings, he did not say how many evenings it had saved. When someone accused the archive of coldness, he did not point to its anti-shame laws. A defense might be relevant later. First, the harmed account had to remain unconverted long enough to answer the architecture.

Neris introduced a constitutional change: no internal category could be the only route by which that category was challenged. The body, the affected person, outside expertise, practical consequence, and the descendant all received standing.

Senn moved several treasured diagrams into the Echo Gallery. “Coherent,” he wrote beneath them. “Not independent evidence.”

Hadrin removed a wall between the Live House and the Cold Hall so visitors could see current doctrine beside its revisions.

Mara created rooms where no audit occurred.

The keepers fought it for six days. Hadrin fought it hardest, and he fought it because he had built the shelving with his own hands and knew exactly which joints were load-bearing, and he stood up in the Live House and said that a man who takes a wall out of a building in order to prove his humility is not humble, he is standing in the rain.

“Then tell us which wall,” Neris said.

“That is the point. I do not know. Nobody knows until it comes down.”

“Then we will take one down and see.”

They took one down and the roof held, and Hadrin went up on the roof himself to check, and came down, and said nothing, and took down the next one.

The widow whose grief had been preserved so thoroughly that strangers knew its architecture better than she did was named Sabe Corrow, and she did not want an apology, and she said so before anyone could offer her one. She wanted the file. Not sealed. Not amended. In her hands, in the market, to do with as she liked.

Neris gave it to her.

She burned it in the square in front of all four keepers—the town called them the Five Keepers, counting the empty chair of the fifth office—and about two hundred other people, and it took a long time, because a file that thorough does not burn quickly, and nobody helped her and nobody stopped her and nobody said a word.

Senn wrote afterwards that it was the worst hour of his life and that he would defend the decision to his death, and that both of those things were true at once, and that he had no method for holding them together and was going to have to learn one.

The House became smaller.

Finally, he wrote:

The House is legitimate only while it preserves truths, persons, goods, and futures that it does not own.

Mara read the sentence.

“Do not make it eternal.”

Eiren wrote the date beside it.
