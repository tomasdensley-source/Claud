# Chapter 7 — The Council of Water

*The young at the condemned monument*

The council returned to the field at midsummer with survey cords, iron stakes, and a machine inherited from an age that had forgotten to leave instructions.

The machine was a dark cylinder on three legs. When placed on level ground, a glass eye turned by itself and drew a blue line through the air. No one in Verrin knew how to make such a device. The council kept it wrapped in wool and called its use a municipal tradition rather than a miracle.

Councilor Odran drove the first stake beside a buried pillar.

Sister Ilya objected. “The covenant forbids cutting the god’s court.”

“The covenant also commands clean water.”

“It commands the care of what carries memory.”

Odran looked at the ruined legs. “Then memory has already failed. We cannot repair a face no one has seen.”

“Perhaps the duty begins where remembering becomes difficult.”

“That sentence can preserve every ruin forever.”

Neither was foolish. That made the dispute dangerous.

The council hired Hadrin to inspect the buried stone. He refused at first, then accepted after Odran showed him how the old conduit aligned beneath the throne.

“If the conduit still carries water,” Odran said, “the new works will cost half as much.”

Hadrin’s first trench uncovered a pillar twelve paces long, carved with lines so fine they looked like writing. At sunset the lines began to glow faintly beneath the dirt. They were thin as hair and arranged in repeating knots. Hadrin crouched over them until his knees hurt. He touched one with the back of a finger. Light traveled beneath the soil and surfaced twenty paces away.

“Can it carry water?” Odran asked.

“Yes.”

“What else flows through stone?”

Hadrin looked toward Sister Ilya, then toward the crowd gathering at the edge of the trench. “Meaning, if enough people behave as though it does.”

Only the first answer entered the public report.

Nothing false was written. The omitted uncertainty turned the pillar network into infrastructure available for reuse rather than sacred architecture whose function remained unknown.

He asked Odran to record both statements.

“A council report cannot preserve every superstition spoken beside a trench.”

“Hadrin did not call it superstition.”

“He also did not explain it.”

“Then record that he could not explain it.”

Odran’s patience thinned. “A town cannot govern by every uncertainty.”

“A town should not govern by deleting uncertainty either,” Eiren said.

For the first time, Odran looked at Eiren not as a boy in the crowd but as an obstacle with a future.

The town divided without admitting it had divided. Practical people wore a small blue cord for the waterworks. The devout tied white thread around their wrists. Torren wore yellow.

“What does yellow mean?” Eiren asked.

“It means I do not need an elder to choose my color.”

That night, someone painted a yellow crown on the throne.

Eiren suspected Torren until he saw Torren’s face. Torren was delighted in public and pale when nobody watched. He had painted a small crown on the seat. He had not painted the network now growing through every fracture of the god’s legs.

“Tell them it wasn’t me,” he whispered.

“Will you tell them what was?”

Torren looked at the crowd cheering beneath the yellow lines.

“They finally think I’m brave,” he said.

By morning the crown had spread across the remaining legs, not as paint spreads but as ivy does. Fine golden lines entered every crack.

Torren called it proof that the monument approved of mockery.

Sister Ilya called it blasphemy.

Eiren called it nothing. Naming a thing too early was another way of giving it a throne.
