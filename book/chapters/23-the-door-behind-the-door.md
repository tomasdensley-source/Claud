# Chapter 23 — The Door Behind the Door

For three days, no door in Verrin led reliably to the room behind it.

The first day of wrong doors produced inconvenience. The second produced terror.

A baker opened his oven and found rain falling through a moonless shaft. A child entered a schoolroom and emerged from a house whose inhabitants had never heard of Verrin. A widow opened her wardrobe and saw her husband standing in a corridor, younger than on the day he died, calling a name she could not hear.

The keepers established door stations. No one crossed alone. Before opening, each traveler spoke their name, destination, and one memory another person could verify. The ritual seemed absurd until people who skipped it returned unable to remember where they belonged.

Eiren and Mara searched for a missing carpenter through twelve kitchens, three cellars, an orchard at night, and a narrow office lit by white panels that hummed overhead. The office contained rows of empty desks and papers printed with symbols neither could read. Nothing in it had been made for living people, yet every chair faced forward as though awaiting them.

“It feels finished for a purpose that did not include us,” Eiren said.

At the far end, the missing carpenter stood before a wall, repeating his address. First the house went, then the street, then the town, then the word world itself. By the time they reached him, he could remember only home.

Mara spoke his wife’s name. Eiren described the blue door he had built. The wall acquired a handle.

They brought him back.

Alone in the lower spaces, a person could lose the network through which a place meant home.

The cracked conduit beneath the throne could not be repaired with Verrin tools. Hadrin tried steel clamps, resin, stone sleeves, and pressure bands. Each held for an hour, then appeared beside the break as though the work had been moved rather than undone.

Sister Ilya searched the oldest liturgies. One passage described the Court Beneath the Court, where the First Ones descended to mend the Wall from its unwitnessed side.

“A myth,” Odran said.

“A map,” Eiren replied.

The entrance appeared behind a pantry door in the One House.

Eiren opened it expecting shelves. Beyond stood a corridor with low yellow walls, damp carpet, and white panels humming in the ceiling. The architecture was offensively ordinary. It lacked the dignity of a tomb and the strangeness of a dream. It looked like a place built for work no one wished to remember.

The rope around Eiren’s waist went slack after six steps, though Mara still held the other end in the pantry.

“Come back,” she said.

Her voice reached him from a great distance.

On the corridor wall someone had written:

**NO PERSON SHALL DEPEND UPON ONE WITNESS ALONE.**

The letters were the same as those in his book.

He returned and found that only one breath had passed in the pantry. “We have to go through,” he said. Mara looked at the corridor. “We?”
