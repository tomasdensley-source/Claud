# Chapter 28 — The Three Choices of Years

*The three choices of years*

The gods who chose different measures of time The Chronicle divided the First Ones not by nation or creed, but by their answer to time. The Chronicle showed the three choices through lives rather than laws.

One founder, Eda Morrow, entered the Calix with silver hair and requested that her body continue aging. She planted the first orchard, raised two nativeborn children, and died while the youngest held her hand. Her death became the model for the Counting of Years. The record preserved grief, inheritance, and the deliberate refusal to become an everlasting parent above every generation.

Another, Jal Sen, refused the Count. After ten thousand years, at a covenant hearing, a young archivist asked who had written the mercy clause everyone else attributed to him. Jal recited the clause, its amendments, and the room in which it had been drafted, but not the name of the woman who drafted it. The Chronicle supplied what he would not: Sorel An. She had been dead nine thousand six hundred years.

“Say her name,” the archivist told him.

Jal looked at the empty chair beside his own. “No.”

His continuity had once steadied cities and corrected histories before they hardened into legend; now every name opened onto too many dead. Soon he stopped using any of them. He entered the lower halls and did not return.

A third, Amara Vey, lived without age for centuries and then asked for mortality. The request was treated as illness by some immortals and sacrament by others. She chose wrinkles, weakness, forgetting, and death not because life had become worthless, but because finitude had become the form in which love could again be offered rather than accumulated.

The people of Verrin had inherited all three stories as myths of gods who accepted time, rejected it, or returned it. The Chronicle revealed no contradiction between the myth and the history.

Torren’s yellow movement had mocked the stories as primitive explanations for natural death. The truth was stranger: mortality had been authored as a choice inside a world where death was no longer inevitable.

Eiren wondered which choice he would have made.

Mara answered without being asked. “You would have postponed choosing until the question became architecture.”

The remark contained affection and indictment in equal measure.

One founder left a statement in the Chronicle:

I chose forever because I feared losing the world. I choose death because forever has made me unable to receive it.

Eiren recognized the seated god from the field.

His name had been Cael Oran. He had refused the Count. He had founded the court around which Verrin later grew. He had lived long enough to be remembered in several incompatible forms. In one record he was merciful. In another, absolute. In another, absent for centuries while his covenant continued to govern people who could no longer question him.

He had not chosen death.

He had descended into the lower halls.

The statue was not a tomb.

It was the memory of a departure.
