# Chapter 18 — The Play in Yellow

*The actor who mistook the center for a crown*

The play had no announced author.

Its first audience included people who had not entered a temple in years and people who had never missed a rite. Both laughed at the same jokes. The keepers were portrayed as frightened custodians of locked cupboards. Every prohibition concealed either incompetence or appetite. Every elder who said not yet was revealed, one scene later, to mean never, unless I remain in charge.

The satire succeeded because enough of it was true.

Eiren recognized officials who had hidden uncertainty behind ceremony. Mara recognized rules enforced long after their purpose had died. Sister Ilya laughed once, sharply, when the false priest used reverence to avoid apologizing. The audience’s relief was genuine. Yellow entered through wounds that had been denied in white.

Its first act appeared as loose pages in taverns. Its second was performed in the dye market by apprentices who claimed they had learned it from travelers. By the third act, everyone in Verrin knew a line or two.

It was called The King Who Needed No Witness.

The story concerned a ruler who discovered that every inherited law had been written by frightened people. He tore down the walls, opened every sealed room, and freed his subjects from reverence. Whenever disaster followed, the play revealed that the disaster had been staged by priests desperate to recover power.

Torren played the King.

He wore a pale mask and a yellow robe. In the final scene he sat on a copy of the ancient throne while actors dressed as keepers begged him not to open a black door.

“Every forbidden door,” the King declared, “is an argument that fears being answered.”

He opened it.

The stage behind him contained no scenery.

For one breath, Eiren saw a corridor extending farther than the town hall could physically contain. A hard white light hummed overhead without flame. The walls were yellowed and damp. A black chair stood alone at an intersection of halls.

Then the curtain fell.

The audience applauded.

When the applause subsided, Sister Ilya remained seated. Torren came down the aisle with the mask beneath his arm.

“The play gives every warning the same grammar: manipulation,” she said. “It teaches the answer before the evidence arrives.”

Torren lifted the mask between them. “Then show them a warning that does not conceal a hand upon the door.”

After the impossible corridor appeared, Torren sat alone backstage with the mask in his lap.

“I didn’t write the last line,” he said.

“Who did?”

“The pages arrive. I improve them.”

“Stop performing it.”

Torren looked toward the hall where the crowd still chanted the King’s speech. “You built a House and they called you a bearer. I built a play and they finally listen.”

“Listening is not the same as being answerable.”

“That sounds like something said by a person whose audience already calls him wise.”

On the storage-room wall, dampness spread into the shape of a door.

A voice from the darkness said, “The play reads the reader.”

They turned. No one stood there.

On the floor lay a page in handwriting neither man recognized. Its edges were older than the paper used anywhere in Verrin. The ink had browned, but one word remained yellow, not colored by pigment so much as fevered from within.

Later.

Beneath it were several lines.

He does not often say, betray. He says, later.

He does not often say, lie. He says, soften it.

He does not often say, abandon love. He says, preserve the mood.

Torren read the lines twice.

“That is good,” he said.

“It is a warning.”

“Warnings can be good writing.”

That night another page appeared beneath his mask.

It contained one sentence.

Later is the first street of Carcosa.
