# Chapter 24 — The Corridor of Delays

*The corridor no blueprint admitted*

Eiren, Mara, Senn, and Neris entered with lamps, rope, chalk, food, and the names of forty witnesses written on their clothing. The old rites required a traveler beneath the Wall to carry more than one account of themselves. Torren came down four hours behind them, alone, with a lamp he had stolen and no names on him at all, and when they found him at the second intersection he said he had come to watch them fail and be able to describe it accurately.

“Go back,” Mara said.

“There is no back. I have already checked, which is more than the four of you did before you came down here congratulating yourselves on your rope.”

Senn put pen to cloth.

“Do not,” Torren said.

“You have to carry more than one account of yourself.”

“I have several hundred. Ask anyone in the field.” But he stood still while Senn tied the cloth to his sleeve, and he did not look at what was written on it, and he did not take it off, and he was still wearing it at the bottom.

At the first intersection, the way back disappeared.

The corridor contained doors labeled in languages no one had spoken in Verrin.

The Corridor of Delays did not threaten them with monsters. It offered reasonable waiting.

One door opened onto a room where Eiren could finish every necessary report before apologizing to Mara. Another offered Senn a complete source chain for Alis’s son, provided he postponed speaking the uncertain name. Neris found a ledger in which every correction could be made without embarrassing a living keeper, if only she waited until each one had retired.

The corridor understood the moral vanity of careful people. It rarely said never. It said after the next clarification.

At the second, they found an inn.

A warm room waited behind its door. The beds were clean. A woman with kind eyes told them no truth improved when handled exhausted. Senn called her the Numbing Innkeeper. They rested one hour and woke three days farther from Verrin.

At another door, a playwright offered to explain their descent as a necessary chapter in Eiren’s growth. His interpretation was beautiful. It made the broken conduit seem purposeful and the town’s danger seem chosen for their development. Mara struck him across the mouth.

“A wound does not owe you a plot,” she said.

The playwright smiled through blood. “Then why did you bring a narrator?”

At the next station, a clerk stamped their maps LATER. The ink spread over every line they had not yet walked. Neris burned the pages before the stamp could reach their names.

An Anemurge was working in the fifth room of the corridor and did not stop when they came in.

The Anemurge looked harmless as it moved along a rack of small glass stoppers, unstopping them one at a time, and the room filled slowly with a warmth like the hour after a good meal, and Neris sat down on the floor without deciding to.

“What is it doing?” Senn asked, and his voice had gone soft and pleasant and slightly slow.

“Nothing,” said the Anemurge. “That is the entire service. I take nothing from you. I add nothing to you. I make the difference between the two stop mattering for a while.”

Mara had her hand around Eiren’s wrist, hard.

“It is not lying,” she said. “That is what frightens me.”

The Anemurge unstopped another bottle. “You are all extremely tired, and you have been carrying an unpayable account down a staircase for two days, and I am offering you one hour in which the account is simply not in the room. I will not take the account. It will be waiting for you. I promise you that it will be waiting.”

“And the hour?” Eiren said.

“The hour comes off the end. Not off mine.”

Neris was already asleep. Torren had his back to the wall and his eyes half shut and an expression Eiren had never seen on him, which was peace.

It was Mara who broke the bottles, and she broke them with the flat of the lamp, and afterwards she sat on the floor among the glass with her hands bleeding and said that she wanted the hour, that she had wanted it more than she had wanted anything in the corridor, and that she would like it recorded that she had not refused it because she was strong.

Senn wrote it down. It is the seventeenth entry in the descent record and it is the only one in his hand that shakes.

Torren was the last to leave that room. Before he followed them out, he pocketed a shard from one of the broken bottles.

The Watcher came after. He recorded each hesitation with perfect accuracy and did nothing.

“Why do you not help?” Eiren asked.

“Observation preserves neutrality.”

“Neutrality has changed.”

The Watcher looked almost pleased. “Then you have met the Glaw.”

At an intersection they found a man seated with a letter on his knees. He had rewritten it so often that the paper had become soft as cloth.

“Who is it for?” Mara asked.

“My brother.”

“Where is he?”

“Behind that door.”

“How long have you been here?”

The man looked at the letter. “I was young when I began.”

The man’s delay had acquired rooms, habits, and a story in which waiting proved seriousness.

Mara knocked on the door. The man cried out as though she had struck him.

“I am not ready.”

“Then tell him that,” she said.

The brother opened. The first sentence was poor. The apology contained explanation where it needed grief. The conversation did not heal thirty years. But the corridor behind them shortened by one turn.

The corridor grew longer whenever they postponed a decision.

The corridor ended at a gate of black stone, and beyond the gate the sky was yellow.
