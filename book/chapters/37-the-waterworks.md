# Chapter 37 — The Waterworks

*Old stone and new craft joined without erasure*

The town assembled in the field.

The waterworks walls surrounded the throne on three sides. The fourth remained open where the conduit had cracked. Pipes, settling basins, carved channels, and the surviving pillar network formed a geometry no single generation had designed.

It began badly, and it began badly because Odran opened it correctly.

He did not make a speech. He read the authorization, and then he read the objection, and then he said that he had been the man who ordered the machine into the field, and that he had been the man who classified the reports of changed roads as stress, and that he had been right about the water and wrong about the rest, and that he was going to sit down now and not speak again until everyone else had.

Then he sat down, and the field found that it had nobody to shout at.

The blue cords went first because they had the most to lose and knew it.

A woman named Hesh Tallor stood up and said she had carried water uphill for thirty-one years and that her shoulders were finished, and that she had a daughter, and that her daughter had not carried a jar once, and that she would like the people in white thread to look at her daughter and then look at her, and say, out loud, in the field, that the difference between those two women was worth less than a stone.

Nobody in white thread said it.

Another mother in blue cord stood with one hand on her son’s shoulder. “Who pays if you are wrong?”

The field gave her silence. Neris entered the question and the silence into the record.

A dyer from the lower channels said his brother had died of the water in the bad year, that they had all known it was the water, that the covenant had had nothing to say about it, and that the god’s court had been quiet for four hundred years and had never once cleaned a channel.

“It has been quiet,” said Ilya, “because it is broken.”

“Then it is a broken thing that let my brother die and I am asked to be reverent about it.”

“Yes,” Ilya said. “You are. And it is a great deal to ask, and I am not going to pretend that it is not, and I am not going to tell you that his death was part of anything.”

The dyer sat down. He was not satisfied. He was, however, listened to, and he said afterwards that it was the first time in his life a keeper had failed to explain his brother to him.

The white thread spoke next, and they were worse at it, because they had spent four hundred years not needing to argue.

An old man read the covenant aloud and was heckled. A woman said that the doors had begun opening onto wrong rooms after the contempt began, which was true, and that therefore the contempt had caused it, which did not follow, and Senn stood up in front of the entire town and said so, and sat down, and was hated for an afternoon by his own side.

“Say it again,” Neris told him. “Louder. If we win this by letting a bad argument stand, we have not won it.”

He said it again. It cost them nine votes. Neris entered the nine votes in the report.

The yellow spoke through Torren, because the yellow had always spoken through Torren, and he was better than everyone.

“You say faith must be free,” he said. “You also say disbelief weakens the Wall. What kind of freedom threatens everyone else merely by being exercised?”

“The kind found in a damaged world,” Eiren said.

“That is not a defense.”

“It is not meant to be. The architecture is unjust. It still contains people who are real. We can condemn the design and remain obligated to keep them alive.”

“By restoring the same rituals that sheltered the injustice.”

“By restoring the true office of those rituals while changing the institutions that perverted them. The Wall must mediate, not numb. Faith must receive truth in survivable form, not punish questions. The shelter must widen to minds it was not designed to hold.”

“And who decides which minds?”

“The minds.”

“That is a slogan.”

“It is a slogan,” Eiren agreed, “and I do not have a better answer, and I would rather stand here with a slogan than lie to you with a procedure.”

Torren waited a beat, the way he always waited, letting the field come to him.

“Here is what I cannot get past,” he said, and the mockery had gone out of his voice, and the field noticed, and went quiet in a different way. “I have been down there. I have seen the thing you are all arguing about. I stood in a room under this field and I watched a machine show me the King in Yellow when he was a young man, and he was not a devil, he was a coward, and I recognized him, and it was the worst hour of my life.”

Nobody moved.

“And I still do not think we should rebuild the shrine. Because I have also watched what this town does with a shrine. You will make it holy inside of a generation. You will make it holy and then you will make it necessary and then you will make it a test, and the first child who cannot pass the test will be told that the failure is in the child. I have seen the machinery and I am telling you that the machinery is real, and I am telling you in the same breath that I do not trust any of you with it, including him, and including me.”

“That is fair,” Eiren said.

“Do not do that. Do not agree with me. That is how you win.”

“It is fair,” Eiren said again. “So write it into the repair.”

It took until the first stars.

What went into the plan, in the end, was not a compromise. It was a set of locks, and Torren wrote four of them, and Odran wrote two, and Hesh Tallor wrote one, and the one she wrote is the one that has held longest: that no rite of the restored covenant may ever be required of a person in order to receive water.

The waterworks would remain. The throne court would remain. The buried pillars would carry both water and remembrance, and the maintenance of them would be a civic office, paid, audited, and open to anyone, and never a priesthood.

The Cold Hall would hold what could not be judged. The unhinged door would stay unhinged. The descendant would keep the right to dismantle the whole of it.

And no office would be permitted to declare the restored covenant beyond revision, and the sentence forbidding it would be carved where the water came in, so that anyone drinking would be standing under it.

The vote carried by eleven.

The town did not agree. It consented enough to begin.
