# Chapter 42 — The Years Without Him

*A town that does not know what it is standing on*

The water ran clean.

That was the first thing, and for a long time it was the only thing, and nobody in Verrin thought about it any more than they thought about the fact that the sun came up. Children were born who had never carried a jar uphill. The dye channels ran clear enough to see the bottom, and the dyers complained about it, because clear water showed every fault in a batch.

In the second year the council voted funds to repair the field. Nobody could say precisely what had happened there. There had been a hearing, and there had been a repair, and the old conduit had been rejoined to the new works, and the Wall, whatever the Wall had been, had stopped failing.

The minutes of the hearing survived. They recorded that a proposal had been made, that it had been contested, that Councilor Odran had opened the session and had not closed it when it turned against him, and that the plan had been adopted by a margin of eleven.

The minutes did not record who had made the proposal. The line was there. The name was not. It had been written and the ink was on the page and the letters had gone, in the way that a footprint in a road goes: not scrubbed out, merely walked over until there was nothing to see.

The clerks assumed a fault in the ink.

Mara did not go to the field for eleven months. Then she went every day for two years, and then she went on the eighth of every month, and then she went when she felt like it, which was often.

She sat on the stone. She did not talk to it, because she had watched what happened to people who talked to stones, and because she had a horror of becoming the widow of a man the town could not remember, standing in a field, being kind to a rock while people brought her food and looked at each other.

So she sat, and she worked. She brought the birch labels. She brought the pots. She had two apprentices by then and neither of them would sit in the field with her and both of them thought it was a superstition and she let them think so.

In the fourth year she got the red.

She wrote it out, the way she had said she would, and she hung it on the wall of the market where any fool could read it: the eleven days, the temperature of the water, the beetles, the pear trees on the north slope, the whole of it, in a sentence a child could follow. Within five years there were a hundred people making it and the price was nothing. She was one of them. She was not rich.

The colour was still here.

Senn grew old and stopped mistrusting agreement, and had to be taken off the Convergence bench by Neris, who wept about it and did it anyway, and who was by then a hard woman of sixty with a reputation for being impossible in a way that people had learned to be grateful for.

Arda kept the House. She kept the empty chair empty. She kept the Bronze Guard polished. She refused, three times, to allow the House to be called a religion, and the third time she did it in front of a delegation from the coast who had come specifically to found one, and she was extremely rude to them, and Mara laughed for a week.

Nobody could say who had built the House.

This was not a mystery to anyone. It was simply not a question. The House had been built the way a road is built, by people, over time, and the keepers were the keepers, and the discipline was the discipline, and if you had asked a child in Verrin who had begun it, the child would have looked at you the way a child looks at an adult who has asked which person invented bread.

The Cold Hall filled slowly. Sera Loam. Bern of the dye row. Lio, in the sealed room, unread. Ilya, on the first night of every year, four hundred voices. Rian, whom nobody could describe and everybody could name, and whose blue coat was in the ground under a pear tree that Asha had chosen and had lived just long enough to see planted.

The stone in the field stayed warm in winter.

The keepers knew this. Hadrin noted it in the maintenance records, and after Hadrin died his apprentice continued the notation in the tone reserved for facts about masonry.

Children played on it. It was the only warm stone in the field in January and they were not fools.

Mara died in the nineteenth year.
