# Chapter 26 — The Collector of Unpaid Evenings

*The arithmetic that could not be reversed*

The way out of Carcosa passed through an archive of lives corrected out of memory.

At the far end waited the Collector of Unpaid Evenings.

It carried no weapon. It presented Eiren with the specific hours he had taken from Mara by converting their life into material before it was lived. Each hour had compounded into distance, caution, and the habit of speaking to him as though a clerk stood between them.

“Can these be repaid?” he asked.

“No,” said the Collector.

“Then what is the purpose of the account?”

“To prevent the irreparable from becoming invisible merely because it cannot be reversed.”

Eiren turned to Mara.

“I am sorry.”

The apology did not restore the evenings. It changed what would happen next.

Rooms stood open on both sides. In one, a table was set for five though the family remembered four. In another, a child’s drawings showed a woman no adult could name. A workshop contained tools worn to the shape of hands that had never existed. None of these remains were complete enough to defeat the correction. They were friction: the world failing to close smoothly over what it had removed.

Senn stopped before a small bedroom.

“This belongs to Alis’s son,” he said.

There was no evidence for the statement.

He entered anyway.

On the wall, scratched at the height of a child, were the words MOTHER REMEMBER ME.

The Collector stood in the doorway.

“What was his name?” Eiren asked.

“No witness remains who can answer.”

“Then why does the room remain?”

“The correction was incomplete.”

Eiren opened his book. The blank token fell from between the pages.

A name appeared upon it: Lio.

Senn spoke the name aloud.

The room changed. A wooden horse appeared beneath the bed. Ash gathered in a bowl. Somewhere far above, perhaps in Verrin, a woman began screaming with the return of grief.

Eiren wanted to stop the process.

“Can remembrance harm the person it saves?” he asked the Collector.

“Every return restores cost as well as presence.”

“Then should we have left him gone?”

“The account does not decide which pain is preferable.”

Senn held the token. “Lio,” he said again.

A child appeared in memory but not in body: hair cut badly by his father, a chipped tooth, a habit of asking whether rain remembered the sea. The image passed through all five travelers and entered the room. The room stabilized.

The Collector did not interfere.

“Why allow this?” Mara asked.

“The account is not the judge,” it said. “It keeps what happens.”

They continued.

The next room was an ordinary dining room with a cup of milk cooling beside an empty chair.

A blue line circled the little clay pot on the table.

Eiren stopped so suddenly Mara struck his shoulder.

The room contained a wooden bird with one pear-wood wing larger than the other. A blue coat hung beside the door. Under the table lay a scrap of paper bearing the first letter of Eiren’s name.

He knew none of these objects.

His body knew them.

His hand reached for the lintel and touched it twice.

The gesture opened a small violence in memory: a man arriving late with a child on one shoulder, a joke about geese, Grandmother Asha threatening to put someone in the stew. The face remained blurred, but the room acquired warmth and the warmth acquired grief.

“Rian,” Eiren said.

The name did not come from the archive. It came from Eiren’s own mouth.

The correction resisted. The cup became a serving cup. The blue coat altered color. The bird’s second wing smoothed until it looked factorymade. Mara seized Eiren’s wrist.

“Tell me one true thing.”

“He repaired small things badly enough that everyone could see where love entered the work.”

Senn repeated the sentence.

Mara added, “He touched every lintel twice.”

The room shook. Far above them, an old woman woke weeping beside a folded blue coat.

No body returned. No complete history reassembled. The correction had gone too far. But the name remained in three living minds and one private record.

Rian had not been restored to the town.

He had been prevented from becoming perfectly absent.

The Collector looked at Eiren. “A system may erase a life and still fail to erase the debt incurred by erasing it.”

“Why was he removed?”

The Collector pointed toward the downward passage.

“Below this point you will learn why the world calls some minds waste, why the First Ones were not corrected, why the King in Yellow remains after cities forget their builders, and why the world prefers some truths to remain sacred.”

Beyond the Collector, enormous mechanisms turned without shape or sound.

“What is the judge?” Eiren asked.

The Collector pointed downward again.

There lay the Chronicle of the First Ones and the true name of the world.
