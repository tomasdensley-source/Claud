# Chapter 39 — The Names That Began to Fade

Sister Ilya died in the third week of the fading, in her own bed, of being eighty-one.

There was nothing strange about it. That was the strangeness. In a season when doors opened onto the wrong rooms and men forgot their brothers, the keeper of the town’s names went out of the world the ordinary way, in the afternoon, with the window open, complaining about the noise from the market.

She had been the first person in the book to say that *what has no witness returns to the unmade*. She had said it to nine-year-olds, in a cold room, with a page of ink in her hand.

The House was very busy that week.

The repair required seven days of continuous remembrance.

Every household in Verrin carried names to the field. They spoke ancestors, strangers, failures, missing people, and those whose histories had become uncertain. They did not praise without accuracy. One person remembered a kindness, another a cruelty, another an unfinished promise. The purpose was not to polish the dead into icons, but to provide enough truthful relation that existence could retain shape.

For the dead, a household read the papers from the person’s living witness-pot over the name-bowl, then burned the papers and filled the pot with earth. Only then did the safeguard for the living become a memorial vessel.

The first day of remembrance was orderly. Families arrived with prepared names and clean stories. By the third day, exhaustion stripped away performance. People forgot dates, contradicted one another, and admitted that some ancestors had harmed them. The rite became more truthful as it became less beautiful.

One household spoke a flattering falsehood into the pillars, saying their father had never raised his hand in anger. The line under their street darkened for one night.

Alis spoke Lio’s name publicly for the first time. She remembered his chipped tooth and his cruelty to insects. She refused to let the recovered child become a saint merely because erasure had almost taken him.

Torren came without yellow cloth. He stood before the throne and said, “I made contempt feel like courage because I wanted reverence to lose the power to shame me.” The confession did not absolve him. It restored the relation between his choice and what followed. Odran named every emergency measure he had allowed to become permanent.

Bern and Loris, the dye-house brothers, stood together and remembered the repeating alley they had marked with yellow thread; Bern said Loris had tied the better knot, and Loris corrected him.

Eiren went to his grandmother’s house before the rite, because Neris had told him to and because Mara had told him to and because he had been putting it off for nine days and had noticed himself doing it.

Asha was ninety. She sat by the fire with a blanket over her knees and a bowl in her lap, and she had not made the bowl and could not have said whose it was. He said: there was a man. He came to this house. He mended things and he mended them badly, and he was here often enough that a place was set for him, and the world has taken him out, and his name was Rian.

She did not say anything for a while.

Then she said, “Under the bed.”

It was a blue coat, folded, with the dust of four years on the top fold and none in the creases.

“I did not know why I kept it,” Asha said. “I have known for four years that I was keeping a coat for a reason and I could not find the reason, and I have been very frightened, and I did not tell anybody, because at my age they take the house away for less.”

“Rian.”

“Rian,” she said, and she said it the way a person says a word in a language they have not spoken since they were small: badly, and then correctly, and then over and over.

Asha pressed her palm into the dust on the fold. “Your House erased my son. That was unforgivable, and there is nothing you can do that will pay for it.”

Eiren stood with his hands at his sides.

“I forgive you,” she said. Then she gathered the coat against her chest.

She would not let him take the coat to the field. She carried it herself, the whole way, and she would not be helped, and she was the one who put it in.

Mara’s memories of Eiren became shorter. On the fifth day she could no longer remember their first kiss until he described the berry stains on her mouth. On the sixth she knew she loved him but could not locate the beginning of the love. The feeling remained after its history loosened.

“Is that better or worse?” she asked.

“Neither. It is what is happening.”

“You always answer that way when there is no repair.”

“Because naming pain a lesson is another form of theft.”

She held his hand while speaking one true thing at a time. The pillar network brightened under each particular memory. Generic praise produced little light.

By dawn of the seventh day, the Wall stood visible around the horizon, beautiful and broken.

The buried pillars awakened beneath the grass.

Lines of light moved from house to house. The old conduit drew water upward through the new settling basins. The Wall appeared on the horizon as a shimmering veil full of cracks.

Eiren walked among the speakers, carrying the repair pattern in his mind.

His mother passed him and did not recognize him.

His sister asked whether he needed help finding his household.

Hadrin remembered teaching a young carpenter but not the student’s name or face.

Senn began each hour by rereading a card: Eiren Vale. Builder of the One House. Witness of the Calix. Do not let the system make uncertainty into consent. By the seventh day, he could read the words but no longer connect them to the man beside him.

Mara remembered longest.

She carried no card. She told him one true thing at a time.

“You hate onions but eat them when someone else cooks.”

“You count turns when afraid.”

“You built a drawer for the unknown and then tried to organize what belonged in it.”

“You apologized too late.”

“You returned.”

Each sentence held him for a little while.

At sunset on the sixth day, Torren approached.

“I remember sitting on the throne,” he said. “I remember you warning me. I do not remember your name.”

“Then remember the warning.”

“Was I the cause?”

“No single act caused the failure. Hubris opened your eyes. The Wall was already weak.”

“Can I repair what I spread?”

“Not by becoming ashamed enough. Carry the cost that remains possible.”

Torren removed the last yellow thread from his wrist and joined the speakers.

He remembered the god Cael Oran as a man who had refused the Count and then went down to mend what he could no longer bear to rule. The statue’s missing torso glimmered for one breath.

The House sent two keepers to Alis and neither of them was Eiren, on Neris’s insistence, because the man who has recovered a thing should not be the man who asks whether you want it.

They put three papers on her table.

The first would enter Lio in the public record, with his name, his age, the manner of his removal, and the date the House had recovered him. It would be read at the Calling. Strangers would say his name in a field.

The second would enter him in the Cold Hall, sealed, where nothing was read aloud and nothing was public, and where his mother could go and sit with the door shut for as long as she liked.

The third was blank. It burned the token, unmade the record, and returned Alis to a world in which she had, for nine years, been a woman who wept about nothing.

“That one is real?” she said. “That one is genuinely on the table?”

“That one is genuinely on the table.”

“You went down into the ground and you found my son and you brought his name up out of it, and you will burn it if I say burn it, and you will not argue with me.”

“We will not argue with you.”

Alis sat for a long time with her hand flat on the third paper.

“I want to be sure I understand you,” she said, “because I have spent nine years being handled. If I take the first one, he belongs to your House. He becomes a story you tell about what your House can do. There will be a stone with his name on it and a keeper will stand beside it explaining what it means.”

“Yes.”

“And if I take the third one, he belongs to nobody, including me, because I will not remember him.”

“Yes.”

“And the second one?”

“The second one, he belongs to you.”

She took the second one. She held it for the rest of the afternoon and did not sign it, and the two keepers sat with her and did not hurry her, and it was dark when she put her mark on it.

Then she asked, and this was not on any of the three papers, whether she might be permitted to say his name out loud once, in the field, at the Calling, herself, without it being written down anywhere by anybody.

Neris said yes before the other keeper could look up the form.

On the seventh night, when the Calling had ended and the pots were being carried out and the fires were being kicked apart, Arda stopped in the middle of the field with a pot in her hands.

“We did not say her name.”

The field went quiet in patches, the way a field does, the news moving out from her in a ring.

“Whose?”

“Ilya’s. Seven nights. Four hundred names. We said Rian. We said Lio. We said Bern. We said a man from the eastern villages that nobody here has ever met.” Arda was not crying. She was holding the pot very carefully, as though it had become heavy. “Nobody said Ilya.”

Senn began immediately: the rite had one form for the dead of the year and another for the dead of the week. The second belonged to the Keeper of Home, who was Ilya and therefore unavailable on account of being dead.

“Stop talking,” Mara said.

Senn stopped.

They lit the fires again. It took an hour to find dry wood. Nobody had eaten and nobody left, and the four hundred people who had been walking home turned around in the dark and came back into the field, and it was well past midnight when the first pot went down.

Eiren did not speak first. He had a hundred things he could have said and every one of them was a sentence, and he had learned what his sentences did to a thing while it was still warm.

It was a woman from the dye row who spoke first, and what she said was that Ilya had once walked six miles in hard rain to return a half-sack of flour that had been counted twice, and had not mentioned it, and had been furious for a month when somebody found out.

That was Asha’s story. Asha had told it about herself, once, at a table with a cup of milk on it.

“She got it from Asha,” the woman said. “She told it to us about Asha for thirty years, every year, and last winter she told it and she got the name wrong, and she said me, and then she stopped, and she looked at us, and she said, well, it was one of us.”

The pot went into the ground.

They said her name four hundred times before it got light.

That is why Lio’s name is spoken in Verrin every year and does not appear in the public record, and every new clerk asks why, and is told.
