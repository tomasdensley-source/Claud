1. **CONTESTED.** Habitual and original-sin traditions affirm it, but act-only accounts of sin deny that a standing condition is sin in the proper sense.

2. **DEMONSTRATED.** Authorship is a fixed fact once the act is done, so the work stays available as a later object of relation.

3. **CONTESTED.** What the knowledge is required for is left unstated, and facing a work may also require knowing its character (for example, its wrongness), not only that it is one's own.

4. **DEMONSTRATED.** Avoidance presupposes knowing what is avoided, so one can know a work is one's own while declining to attend to it.

5. **DEMONSTRATED.** Both are orientations of one agent toward one object, contrary in that same respect; apparent ambivalence splits into different aspects of the work.

6. **DEMONSTRATED.** One cannot face a work *as* one's own without knowing that it is one's own.

7. **DEMONSTRATED.** One can examine a work closely while disowning it, so attention does not entail acknowledgment.

8. **DEMONSTRATED.** Since attending without acknowledging is possible (7), facing it "as one's own" must add acknowledgment.

9. **DEMONSTRATED.** Remorse that stalls short of owning the deed (attrition, self-excusing sorrow) is common and recognizable as incomplete repentance.

10. **CONTESTED.** Repentance is often held to include more than the facing, such as sorrow, resolve to amend, and turning to God, so strict identity is disputed.

11. **CONTESTED.** Theologies of grace affirm it, while autonomy-based accounts of moral self-reckoning deny that it can be given.

12. **FALSE AS STATED.** Grace standardly names the giver's favor (*favor Dei*) as well as the gift, so the exclusion "not the giver's favor" misdescribes the term.
