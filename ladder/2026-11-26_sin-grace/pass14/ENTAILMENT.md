# Pass 14. Entailment conditionals (operator construction; each tested in its own fresh context)

| ID | Exact sentence |
|---|---|
| E-1 | Given these definitions: (1) To face something is to turn toward it rather than away from it, whether for a moment or as a standing. (2) To remain turned away from something is to hold a turning from it, rather than toward it, as a standing over time, and not to be in the course of turning back. (3) To stand turned toward something is to hold a turning toward it as a standing over time, which persists without continuous attention to it. (4) The contrary of a standing is a standing that cannot hold together with it in the same subject, toward the same object, at the same time, though both can be absent. (5) To hold a work as one's own is to acknowledge oneself as its author inwardly and, wherever one speaks of it, in speech as well, neither disowning it nor laying it on another in either. (6) Acknowledging a work of one's own is holding it as one's own and naming it for what it is. (7) A deed is rightly called for by a work of one's own only if it can rightly be done and either answers, in proportion, something the work did that is not yet answered, to those it touched or in one's own conduct, or carries the work's good or its lesson to others. Then standing turned toward a work of one's own, in acknowledgment and in the deeds it rightly calls for, is the contrary of remaining turned away from that work. |
| E-2 | Given these definitions: (1) Attention is holding something in view; it can be close and sustained while one disowns the thing, distorts it, or does nothing it calls for. (2) To hold a work as one's own is to acknowledge oneself as its author inwardly and, wherever one speaks of it, in speech as well, neither disowning it nor laying it on another in either. (3) Acknowledging a work of one's own is holding it as one's own and naming it for what it is. (4) To stand turned toward something is to hold a turning toward it as a standing over time, which persists without continuous attention to it. Then attention alone never amounts to standing turned toward a work of one's own in acknowledgment of it. |
| E-3 | Given these definitions: (1) Repentance is a compound of separable elements, such as sorrow, resolve to amend, and the facing of what one did, so it can contain the contrary of a condition without being identical to that contrary. (2) Repentance is the turning toward the grace that freely gives a maker the standing toward his own work. (3) To stand turned toward something is to hold a turning toward it as a standing over time, which persists without continuous attention to it. Then repentance contains sin's contrary, the standing turned toward one's own work, only once it comes to that standing. |
| E-4 | Given these definitions: (1) One is among what God made, and one can turn toward oneself as a whole, as both what God made and what one has made of it, though not every part of oneself can be seen. (2) Facing God's creation can mean facing what God made for what it is, or facing it also as God's work; only the second faces it as creation. (3) Repentance is the turning toward the grace that freely gives a maker the standing toward his own work. Then repentance can turn toward God's creation as God's work, and toward oneself within it as a whole, though not every part of oneself can be seen. |
| E-5 | Given these definitions: (1) Sin is the abandonment, after understanding, of one's own work: remaining turned away from it. (2) The work of one's own hand is what one has authored: what one has made or done, directly or through others acting at one's direction, or has left undone when it was one's to do; it does not include what another's free choice makes of it, and it remains one's own after it is finished. (3) To understand a work as one's own is to recognize which work is one's own and to grasp, as far as one can see, what it is and what it does; one who suspects a work is theirs and refuses to find out is not excused by that refusal. (4) To remain turned away from something is to hold a turning from it, rather than toward it, as a standing over time, and not to be in the course of turning back. Then sin is remaining turned away, after understanding, from what one has made, done or left undone as its author. |
| E-6 | Given these definitions: (1) Grace is a free giving whose gift is a maker's standing turned toward his own work. (2) A giving is free when it is not owed to the one who receives it, not compelled from the giver, and not made to secure a return. (3) A standing turned toward one's own work can be given to one by another, at least in part, as being faced and named by another can help one come to face. Then grace is the free giving of a standing turned toward one's own work, and such a giving can occur. |
