2. **CONTESTED.** This is a stipulative redefinition. Mainstream accounts treat sin as transgression of divine law, and nothing here refutes them.

3. **CONTESTED.** It follows only if sin is defined as turning away *after* knowledge. Several traditions recognize sins of ignorance.

4. **DEMONSTRATED.** One can know a thing and then avert from it. Ordinary experience of avoidance confirms this order is possible.

5. **DEMONSTRATED.** Repentance is analytically repentance *of* something. Without prior sin there is nothing to repent.

6. **FALSE AS STATED.** Many opposites presuppose what they oppose. Healing presupposes illness, and correlatives presuppose each other (Aristotle's double and half).

7. **FALSE AS STATED.** Sin has at least a contradictory opposite (non-sin) and a contrary opposite (e.g., grace or virtue). Opposition comes in several kinds.

8. **CONTESTED.** Grace is often understood as an unmerited gift, not an orientation of the agent. Placing it in the same logical kind as sin is a substantive assumption.

9. **CONTESTED.** "Turning away" is ambiguous between a motion and a state. The opposite of the motion is turning toward. Only the state (being turned away) is opposed by facing.

10. **CONTESTED.** This holds within the proposed framework. It depends on unproven claims 2, 8 and 9.

11. **FALSE AS STATED.** Inverting a relation yields its converse, not its opposite. The converse of "parent of" is "child of," not an opposite. It is also no general method for finding opposites.

12. **CONTESTED.** Turning back is a change of orientation, and facing is a state. They are distinct as motion and terminus, but calling both "orientations" is questionable, and repentance arguably culminates in facing.
