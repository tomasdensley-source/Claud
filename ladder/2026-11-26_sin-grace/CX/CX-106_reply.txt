**1. BROKEN**

The first half of the conclusion holds. "Grace is the free giving of a standing turned toward one's own work" just restates definition (1). The break is in the second half, "such a giving can occur." Nothing in the premises supports it.

**The gap.** Premise (3) shows only that a standing can be *given* by another, at least in part. Grace needs a *free* giving, which under (2) means three things at once: not owed to the receiver, not compelled from the giver, and not made to secure a return. No premise says a giving of standing can meet all three. So the premises establish the possibility of *a* giving, not of a *free* one.

**The counterexample.** Take a world where premises (1)–(3) all hold as stated, but every giving of standing has strings attached:

- A mentor helps an apprentice face his work only because the mentor's office obliges him to. The giving is owed to the receiver.
- A confessor names a penitent's work only under the rule of his order. The giving is compelled from the giver.
- A friend does it only to earn future loyalty. The giving is made to secure a return.

In this world:
- Grace is still defined exactly as (1) and (2) say.
- (3) is true, because standing really is given by another, at least in part, through being faced and named.

Yet no giving of standing is ever free, so no instance of grace occurs, and on these terms none can. Every premise is satisfied and every word keeps its stated meaning, but "such a giving can occur" is false.

**A secondary weakness.** (3) says standing can be given only "at least in part." The premises never say whether a partial giving counts as "the giving of a standing" in the sense (1) requires. That leaves further room for the conclusion to fail.

To repair the argument, it would need an added premise that some giving of standing can be unowed, uncompelled, and unconditioned. Without that premise, the claim that grace is possible does not follow.
