**Verdict: BROKEN**

**How I read the statement.** It is an identity claim: repentance, as the answer to sin, *is* the turning toward a freely given standing toward one's work. Sin is implicitly defined as remaining turned away from what one has made or done. The clause "whether or not he names it grace" means the object of the turning is fixed by what the gift actually is, not by how the person describes it. An identity claim fails if the defining condition can be met without repentance occurring, or if repentance can occur without the condition.

**Counterexample (the condition is met but repentance does not occur): cheap forgiveness.**

1. A man has wronged his brother. For years he has refused to look at what he did. He is "one who has remained turned away from what he has done."
2. The brother freely forgives him. The forgiveness is offered as standing toward the deed: "I forgive you. Come, and we will face together what you did." This is a free giving, by another, of the standing turned toward that work.
3. The man eagerly turns toward this giving. He seeks it out, accepts the words "I forgive you," and is glad of them. But he takes them only as release. He treats the matter as closed so that he never has to look at it. He does not call it grace. The statement says that does not matter.
4. Every term is satisfied. The man was turned away. He turned toward a free giving. The giving, as given, was of the standing turned toward the work. His naming of it is ruled irrelevant.
5. Yet he remains turned away from his work. The sin, as the statement itself defines it, is unanswered. He has received what Bonhoeffer called cheap grace. That is the opposite of repentance, not an instance of it.

The statement locates repentance in the turning *toward the gift*, not in actually *receiving its content*, so this turning satisfies it. Because it also refuses to let the person's own description of what he turns toward count, it cannot exclude the man who turns toward the gift under the description "escape." The condition is met and the claimed result, an answer to sin, fails.

**Supporting counterexample (repentance occurs without the condition).**

- A man alone on an island has killed his companion. No other person exists to give him anything. Stipulate a world with no God.
- Over years, unaided, he brings himself to face the deed. He grieves without despair, owns it, and amends his life.
- By the ordinary sense of the word, this is repentance, and it ends his being turned away.
- No free giving by God or another is present.

A defender can only answer that some unrecognized gift must have been there, or that self-achieved turning is not "really" repentance. Both replies make the definition true by stipulation rather than by meeting the case. This second example is more open to dispute than the first.

The cheap-forgiveness case alone is enough to break the statement on its own terms.
