**1. BROKEN**

**Counterexample: a past work that calls for a deed because of what it makes possible, not because of anything it did that remains to be answered.**

Suppose that years ago, working alone, I found a simple and safe way to purify contaminated well water. I wrote it up in my own notebook and set it aside. The work wronged no one, harmed no one, and left no debt. It reached no one but me, and nothing in my conduct needs correcting because of it. Now I learn that a nearby village is getting sick from its wells.

My past work rightly calls for a deed: sharing the method with the village. Almost anyone would say this. The reason is that work, the fact that I already made the method, together with the need. That is why the call falls on me and not on a stranger.

Test the statement's necessary condition against this deed:

- **"Answers… what the work did that has not yet been answered."** The work did nothing that awaits an answer. There is no harm to repair, no wrong to own, no debt left open. Sharing the method does not *answer* the work. It *extends* it, carrying its good to new people. The statement's whole frame of answering an outstanding effect has nothing to grip here.
- **"To those it touched."** The villagers were never touched by the work. The deed goes to people outside its reach.
- **"Or in one's own conduct."** One could say any deed is "one's own conduct." But that reading empties the clause, and it is not the statement's meaning. The clause pairs with "to those it touched," so it means answering the work's effects within oneself, such as reform or self-correction. Nothing in me needs answering. The deed goes outward to strangers.
- **"In proportion."** There is no outstanding effect for the deed to be proportioned to. A single private act of discovery could rightly call for a deed of any size, from one letter to years of teaching, depending on how great the village's need is.

All of the statement's terms are kept: it is a past work, of one's own, rightly calling for a deed. Yet the deed meets none of the conditions the statement declares necessary. So the "only if" fails.

**A second case, if one doubts that good works "call":** I once made a costly mistake that harmed only me, and I have fully recovered and changed my habits. Nothing is left unanswered. That past mistake can still rightly call for warning others who are about to make it. This deed again goes beyond the work's own unanswered effects, to people it never touched.

**The statement's best defence** is to stretch "answers" to cover any fitting response to a work's potential, or to read "in one's own conduct" as covering any deed at all. Either move changes what the words mean, and the statement would then rule nothing out. Read as written, it is a principle of reparative or corrective response. As a necessary condition on everything a past work can rightly call for, it is false.
