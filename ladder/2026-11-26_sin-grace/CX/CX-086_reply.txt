# Counterexamples to the Six Statements

These statements are aphoristic and partly metaphorical. I take each one at its own words. Where it makes a universal or defining claim ("X is Y," "the father names his son"), one case that meets its terms and fails its claim is enough to break it. I note where a defender could rescue a statement only by stretching its key word until it no longer excludes anything.

---

## 1. "Shame is the running from the name we've been given. This is the Mind's counterfeit for conscience."

**Verdict: BROKEN**

**Counterexample:** A man is publicly and truthfully named "betrayer." He does not run from the name. He owns it, says "yes, that is what I am," and weeps. Peter after the cock crowed fits this. Two things follow:

- **No running:** He feels shame, but he is not fleeing the name. He is accepting it. So shame is not identical to running from the name.
- **Not counterfeit:** His shame tracks a real wrong and leads him to repentance and restoration. It works as conscience, not as a counterfeit of it.

The reverse case also fails. Someone given a false name ("worthless," a slur) who runs from it is resisting a lie. That is neither shame nor counterfeit conscience.

To rescue the statement, "shame" would have to be redefined so that shame which owns the name doesn't count. That is a stipulation, not a description.

---

## 2. "To atone is to humbly choose to suffer for the love of what is beautiful, and through that charity emerges new creation."

**Verdict: BROKEN**

**Counterexample A (the definition covers too much):** A painter humbly chooses poverty and hardship purely out of love for beauty. She has wronged no one and is reconciling nothing. She meets every term of the definition, yet nothing is being atoned for. Atonement (at-one-ment) requires a breach to repair, and the definition omits it.

**Counterexample B (the promised result fails):** A gardener, out of love for a beautiful old orchard, humbly gives himself through years of drought to keep it alive. The orchard dies anyway and nothing grows in its place. The suffering was chosen, humble, loving, and directed at beauty, but no new creation emerged.

The second clause can be saved only if "new creation" means something invisible that cannot be checked. That makes the claim unfalsifiable rather than true.

---

## 3. "A child implies its mother, and the father names his son."

**Verdict: BROKEN** (by the second clause)

- **First clause holds.** Every human child, including those from IVF, surrogacy, or cloning, has been gestated by and descends from a woman. I found no valid counterexample to it.
- **Second clause fails.** Many sons are not named by their fathers:
  - a posthumous son whose father died in war before the birth;
  - a son whose father abandoned the mother;
  - sons named by mothers or grandparents by custom.

Each is a father and son within the statement's own terms, and the father does not name the son. Since the statement joins both claims with "and," one false conjunct makes the whole false.

---

## 4. "It is beauty that gives the little seed a reason to spring up from below, and it is beauty from which buried things may emerge. She holds without controlling. She receives without owning."

**Verdict: BROKEN**

**Counterexample:** Mung beans sprout in a sealed, pitch-dark drawer on wet paper towels. They spring up from below because of water, warmth, and stored energy, and nothing beautiful is present. A weed germinates in a crack in a toxic, rubble-strewn lot. Buried things also emerge for reasons that have nothing to do with beauty, such as erosion, a bulldozer, or a grave robber.

The statement names a literal "little seed" and assigns beauty as the reason it sprouts. These seeds sprout without it.

A defender could say the seed's own life is the beauty. Then "beauty" means "whatever makes seeds grow," and the claim becomes an empty tautology. The last two sentences ("holds without controlling," "receives without owning") describe beauty's character. They stand or fall with the identification, but they do not rescue it.

---

## 5. "Fate is the weight of receiving the father's name."

**Verdict: BROKEN**

**Counterexample:** Oedipus's fate (to kill his father and marry his mother) was prophesied before his birth, before any name was given. He was then exposed and named by strangers, not by his father. His fate existed before, and apart from, receiving any father's name.

A simpler case: a foundling never named by any father still lives and dies within circumstances he did not choose. He has a fate but never bore the weight of a father's name.

---

## 6. "Faith is to face the morning light, so we may discover again what is beautiful."

**Verdict: BROKEN**

**Counterexample:** Job says, "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him." Mother Teresa reported decades of inner darkness and kept faith throughout. In both cases:

- **No light:** Faith persists precisely in the absence of any morning light. Hebrews 11:1 calls faith "the evidence of things *not seen*."
- **No such purpose:** It is not held so as to rediscover beauty. It holds even when no rediscovery is expected.

The definition also covers too much. A skeptical landscape photographer rises at dawn and faces the morning light in order to find beauty again. He meets every term, and nothing about it is faith.

The statement could be saved by reading "facing the light" as "orienting toward God even in total darkness." But that makes "morning light" mean its opposite, which changes the words' meaning.

---

## Summary

The only claim that survived on its own was "a child implies its mother." Every statement that defines something or makes a universal causal claim failed against a case within its own terms. Each could be rescued only by redefining its central word until it no longer ruled anything out.
