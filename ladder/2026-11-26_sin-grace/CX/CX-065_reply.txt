# Verdicts on the Three Statements

## 1. STANDS

The statement is a modal claim: rebellion does not necessarily amount to sin. To break it, I would have to show that every rebellion against God is sin. That means showing that every rebellion involves remaining turned away, after knowledge, from the work of one's own hand.

The definition does not support this. It locates sin in a person's relation to their own work, not in their stance toward God.

A coherent case of rebellion without sin is easy to build. Take a Job-like or Promethean figure who:

- knowingly defies or accuses God,
- fully faces their own deed as their own, and
- sees the deed for what it is, without evasion.

This person rebels but does not remain turned away from their work. So by the stated definition they do not sin.

To get a counterexample, I would have to redefine rebellion as "turning away from one's own work." That changes the meaning of the terms, so it is not valid. The statement stands.

## 2. STANDS

To break this statement, I would need to show that one of the two conditions entails the other, or that they never overlap. All three required cases can be built within ordinary meanings.

- **Rebellion without turning from grace:** the defiant figure from Statement 1. They protest against God while accepting the facing of their own work.
- **Turning from grace without rebellion:** a devout, obedient person who never defies God. They piously refuse to look at harm they caused and attribute it to "God's will." This is passive evasion with no defiance in it.
- **Overlap:** someone who defies God and also refuses to face what they have done.

The only way to collapse the two would be to stipulate that any turning from grace simply is rebellion. That is a redefinition. The statement stands.

## 3. BROKEN

The grace is defined as "the facing of one's own work as one's own and for what it is." This content requires only two capacities:

- **Self-attribution:** recognizing "this is mine, I did it."
- **Seeing the work accurately:** recognizing "it is cracked, broken, botched."

Neither capacity requires the ability to know right from wrong.

**Counterexample:** imagine a self-aware maker who has agency but no moral faculty. Picture a toddler, before any capacity for moral judgment, or a person whose moral cognition is profoundly absent while their self-recognition is intact. This person knocks over and shatters something they built. They then look at the wreck and fully acknowledge, "I did that, and it is ruined." They face the work as their own and for what it is.

Everything the definition of grace specifies has been received. Yet the receiver cannot know right and wrong.

The statement itself weakens its own requirement. It concedes that moral knowledge need not come before grace, which already separates receiving grace from moral knowing. Its further insistence on moral *capacity* has no basis in the stated content of the grace.

Moral knowledge does matter in the framework, but only through the definition of sin, which turns on what happens "after knowledge." Sin is not the same thing as grace.

**Caveat:** the statement would stand only if "for what it is" were read to mean "including its moral character." The wording does not say that.
