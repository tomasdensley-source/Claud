# Verdicts on the Two Statements on Sin, Grace and Repentance

Both statements share the same definitions of sin and grace. They differ only in how they assign the facing of creation and the facing of oneself. That difference decides the verdicts.

## 1. STANDS

I tried four lines of attack. None produced a case that meets the statement's terms and defeats it.

**Attack (a): sin hidden in an unseen part of oneself.** Suppose a person faces creation as God's work and faces themselves within it as a whole, while some part of them they cannot see holds a wrong work.
- Sin requires being turned away *after understanding*.
- An unseen part is not understood.
- Grace and repentance are both qualified by "as far as these can be seen" and "not every part of oneself can be seen."

So this person is not in sin. The case does not break the statement.

**Attack (b): repentance alongside an understood wrong.** Suppose a person faces themselves as a whole within creation but stays turned away from a wrong they do understand.
- An understood work is a seen part of oneself.
- Facing oneself as a whole, as far as one can see, includes that work.
- So this person is not facing themselves as a whole.

The case is impossible under the statement's own terms.

**Attack (c): mere attention counting as facing.** This is excluded explicitly: "attention alone never reaches that facing."

**Attack (d): "God's work" versus "one's own."** Facing creation as God's work might seem to absorb one's own deeds into God's work. That would conflict with grace, which requires facing them "as one's own." But the statement frames the self as *within* creation. A work can be one's own while one's existence sits inside God's work. The statement never makes these exclusive.

Statement 1 holds together. Because repentance includes facing oneself, it cannot coexist with sin.

## 2. BROKEN

Statement 2 makes repentance *only* "the facing of God's creation as God's work." It moves the facing of oneself to conscience.

**Counterexample:** A person has wronged someone and understands what they did, yet keeps their face turned from that deed.
- By the statement's definition, this is sin.
- The same person contemplates the world with real reverence and faces it, not merely attends to it, as God's work.
- By the statement's definition, this is repentance.
- Nothing in that definition reaches the work of their own hand.

Under Statement 2, this person is fully repentant while remaining in sin, about the very thing they understand.

That is a valid counterexample. It does not need to change the meaning of "repentance." It only needs the word to keep its ordinary sense of turning from sin, and the statement uses "repentance" in the same frame in which it defines sin.

As defined in Statement 2, repentance can coexist with an understood, unfaced sin. It no longer touches sin at all. The work that would make repentance the remedy for sin has been handed to "conscience." What remains under the name "repentance" can be fully satisfied by someone who stays in sin.
