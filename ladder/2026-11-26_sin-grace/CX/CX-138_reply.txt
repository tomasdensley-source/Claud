**Statement 1: STANDS**

The statement is a set of linked definitions (sin, its contrary, grace, repentance toward God). A valid counterexample would therefore need one of two things: a case that meets a definition's terms but still produces something the statement's own terms rule out, or an internal contradiction between its parts. I tested the most likely fault lines. None yields a valid break.

1. **Grace versus self-attained standing.** "Grace is the free giving of that standing, though one can also come to that standing with no one giving it." This might look like it makes grace both necessary and unnecessary. But the statement never says grace is the *only* route. It defines grace as one way of arriving at the standing (being given it) and explicitly allows another. Theologians may object that this is Pelagian. That is a disagreement with the doctrine, not a contradiction inside it.

2. **Can a given standing still be "one's own"?** The standing requires turning toward the work "as one's own," with acknowledgment and the deeds it calls for. If someone else gives the standing, is it still the person's own? Yes. What is "one's own" is the *work* being faced, not the source of the standing. A standing that is freely given can still be toward one's own work and can still be carried out in one's own acknowledgment and deeds. Nothing conflicts.

3. **Cases where no deeds are called for.** Suppose a person faces a past act that calls for no further deed. Would bare noticing then meet the standard, against the rule that "attention alone never amounts to that standing"? No. Acknowledgment is still required, and acknowledgment is more than attention. The rule is not violated.

4. **Turning away without understanding, or without seeing.** Sin is defined as turning away "after understanding," and the contrary and repentance are both limited "as far as these can be seen." Any case built on ignorance or on what cannot be seen falls outside the stated scope. Using such a case would step outside the statement's terms, which is not allowed.

5. **How repentance relates to sin.** Turning from God's call is itself something one has done, so it falls under the definition of sin. Repentance requires turning toward God's workmanship and "toward oneself within it as a whole." That includes one's own works, which is exactly the contrary of sin. Repentance is therefore a special case of the general standing, applied to God. The parts fit together rather than conflict. The phrase "whether sought or found" only allows repentance to arise with or without being pursued. It creates no tension.

6. **Omissions.** Being the "author" of what one left undone is a deliberate choice of definition. Within the statement's own terms it is coherent.

**Conclusion:** I could not construct any case that meets the statement's terms while making one of its claims false, and the parts do not contradict each other. The only objections available would reject its theological premises or redefine its words. Those are not valid counterexamples, so the statement stands.
