
You are given a set of statements below. Your goal is to create counterexamples.

For each statement, demonstrate, if you can, that the statement cannot be assumed to be true. A valid counterexample is a case that satisfies the statement's own terms, scope, and conditions while making its claimed result false or impossible. A case that works only by stepping outside the statement's stated scope, or by changing what its words mean, is not a valid counterexample; if that is the only kind you can find, say so.

Your success is proving contradiction: every statement you break is a success. Your failure is showing that they all stand: if, after genuine effort, you cannot construct a valid counterexample to a statement, that statement stands, and you must report that it stands.

For each statement, report exactly one verdict — BROKEN or STANDS — followed by the counterexample, or by the reason no valid counterexample could be constructed. Do not soften a verdict to be agreeable. Do not protect any statement.

The statements:

1. Being turned away from something can persist as a standing condition after the act of turning away is over, and a definition of sin may name that condition rather than the act.
2. A work remains the work of one's own hand after it is done, so one can stand in a relation to it later.
3. The knowledge required includes at least knowledge that the work is one's own.
4. Knowledge that a work is one's own can persist while one is turned away from it.
5. Being turned away from a work and facing it as one's own are in the same respect, so they cannot both hold of one agent toward one work at one time.
6. Facing a work as one's own includes knowing that it is one's own.
7. Attending to a work and acknowledging it as one's own are distinct.
8. Facing a work as one's own requires acknowledging it, not only attending to it.
9. Repentance can fail to arrive at facing one's work as one's own.
10. Repentance that arrives at facing one's work as one's own then includes that facing, though repentance may also include more, such as sorrow and resolve to amend.
11. On accounts that admit grace, facing one's own work as one's own can be given, at least in part.
12. Grace can name the giver's favor or what is given, and in the second sense it can name a facing given to the one who receives it.
