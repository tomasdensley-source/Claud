**1. BROKEN**

**What the statement claims.** It is an unqualified definition: "Repentance *is* …". It says that every case of repentance has this structure:

- (a) The penitent has already been turned away from his own work, meaning his own effort as the basis of his standing.
- (b) He turns toward grace.
- (c) That grace freely gives the standing his work had been aimed at securing.

A real case of repentance that lacks this structure breaks the definition. Using the word "repentance" in its ordinary sense, including its biblical sense, does not step outside the statement's scope.

**Counterexample 1: Nineveh (Jonah 3).** The text explicitly calls this repentance. The Ninevites "turned from their evil way" and "from the violence that is in their hands."

- What they turned away from was violence and wickedness. They were not turning from reliance on their own work as the ground of standing.
- What they turned toward was ceasing violence and acting rightly. Their own conduct is the content of the turn. They act under an uncertain "who knows whether God will turn," not toward a standing they understand to be freely given.

Condition (a) is absent and (c) is absent, yet this is a paradigm case of repentance.

**Counterexample 2: an ordinary moral case.** A man who has been cruel to his brother comes to grieve it, apologizes, and stops.

- He was never relying on his work for standing, so he was not turned away from it.
- He turns toward his brother and toward better conduct. No grace conferring a standing is involved.

This plainly counts as repentance in ordinary usage, and the definition excludes it.

**Counterexample 3: the call to bear fruit (Matthew 3:8, "bear fruit in keeping with repentance").** Even within the Christian frame, repentance here is directed toward new works rather than away from them. That runs against the definition's core claim that repentance is a turning away from one's own work.

**Why this breaks it.** The definition fits one specific species: the Reformation-style turn from works-righteousness to justifying grace. It presents that species as the essence of repentance, which is the whole genus. Repentance from sin that never involved works-righteousness satisfies the term "repentance" but lacks the defining features. So the statement cannot be assumed true as a general account of what repentance is.
