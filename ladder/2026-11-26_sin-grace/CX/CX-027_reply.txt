# Verdicts on the Two Statements

## Statement 1: STANDS

**Candidate counterexamples and why they fail:**

**(a) The proud owner.** A man commits a wrong and openly says "Yes, I did it, and I'd do it again." He seems to face his work as his own, so the statement would seem to classify him as not in sin. This fails. Statement 1 requires facing the work "in acknowledgment and in deed." To acknowledge a work is to admit what it actually is, a wrong, and not just that one did it. Facing it "in deed" means carrying it and answering for it. The boaster admits authorship only. He faces a flattering version of the act, so he is still turned away from the work as it is. Statement 1 classifies him correctly as in sin.

**(b) Sins of omission.** Someone knows they should help and does not. This seems to involve no "work of one's own hand." But a knowing neglect is still one's own doing. One can face it in acknowledgment and in deed by going back and doing what was left undone. The structure covers the case.

**(c) The moment of commission.** One might object that the original act, and not the later "remaining," is the sin. But someone who acts against what they know is already refusing to face the work as it truly is. The "remaining turned away" can begin at the moment of acting. Pressing the objection further would only replace the statement's definition of sin with a different one, which is not a valid counterexample.

**(d) Humility about a good deed.** Someone does not dwell on their own good work. This person still acknowledges the work and acts consistently with it. They are inattentive, not turned away. The statement itself says attention is not the measure of facing, so lacking attention is not the measure of turning away either.

I found no case that meets Statement 1's own terms while defeating its claims.

## Statement 2: BROKEN

**Counterexample: the proud owner.** A man knowingly ruins a rival. He then declares it openly and repeatedly: "I did that. It is mine. I am glad of it."

- **He is not in sin under Statement 2.** Its sin is being turned away *"refusing to own it."* Whether that phrase explains "turned away" or adds a second condition, he fails it. He refuses nothing. He owns the act completely.
- **He has the contrary of sin.** Statement 2's contrary is only "to stand facing that same work as one's own." He does more than pay attention: he claims the work and stands by it. So he meets the stated requirement that the contrary go beyond attention.
- **The result follows directly.** This unrepentant man has the contrary of sin without any repentance. If his stance was given rather than achieved, the statement's last clause calls it grace.

Statement 2 has no defence against this case. Statement 1 is protected by "acknowledgment and in deed," which requires owning the work for what it is and bearing it. Statement 2 drops that requirement and makes ownership alone the test. A man can own his wrong defiantly without ever turning toward it. The definition therefore puts a clear, continuing sin outside "sin" and labels the defiance itself as the contrary of sin.
