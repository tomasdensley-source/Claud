**Statement 1: STANDS**

I could not build a counterexample that stays inside the statement's terms. Here are the strongest attempts and why each fails.

**Attempt 1: Averting the eyes from temptation.** A penitent avoids looking at the wine, the image or the person that occasions his sin. His return seems complete, yet he refuses to face something God made.
*Why it fails:* He is not refusing to face the thing "for what it is." He avoids it because he does see what it is: a good creature that is dangerous to him. Recognizing it as God's work while declining to put himself near it is facing it, not refusing to. The refusal the statement names is absent.

**Attempt 2: Acceptance by grace before inner work is complete.** The prodigal is embraced before he finishes his speech. The penitent thief is promised paradise. Deathbed conversions are received. Each seems to be a fulfilled return with leftover refusals.
*Why it fails:* Two reasons.
- These cases show that a return can be *received* or *accepted* while incomplete. "Fulfilled" means completed. To treat acceptance as fulfillment is to change what the word means.
- None of these figures is shown willfully refusing some visible creature as God's work. The refusing figure in the parable is the elder brother, whose return is plainly unfulfilled. That supports the statement.

**Attempt 3: Things that are evil or horrifying.** Someone refuses to look at an atrocity, and his return still seems complete.
*Why it fails:* On the statement's own framework, evil as such is not "something God has made." So refusing to accept evil as God's work falls outside the scope. If instead the refused thing is a creature caught up in the evil, such as a victim or a perpetrator, then refusing to see that person as God's work is exactly the unfulfilled return the statement describes.

**Attempt 4: Ignorance or incapacity.**
*Why it fails:* The statement already excludes these. It limits itself to what the person "can see" and to what he "refuses," which implies an act of will. Things that are invisible or not understood are out of scope.

**Why it stands:** The statement is close to analytic. A return toward God means turning the whole will toward God. A deliberate refusal to recognize some visible part of God's work as God's work is a remaining turn of the will away from God. A return that still contains such a refusal is incomplete by the meaning of the terms. Every apparent counterexample works only by:
- reading "fulfilled" as "accepted," or
- reading "refuses to face for what it is" as merely "avoids," or
- treating evil as something God made.

Each of these changes the statement's words or scope.
