## 1. Facing creation "for what it is" versus "also as His workmanship"

**Verdict: STANDS.**

**Attempted counterexample.** Someone faces a thing simply "for what it is." But what it is includes having been made by God. So facing it fully for what it is would already face it as creation, and the first option would also face it as creation. That would contradict "only the second."

**Why it fails.** The statement separates the two senses with the word "also." The first sense is defined as facing the thing apart from its being His workmanship. To count it as facing creation, you must quietly add the workmanship back into the first sense, which changes what the words mean.

"Creation" here is "God's creation," meaning a thing under the description "what God made." That is a relational description. Facing the nature of a thing while leaving its Maker out does not face it under that description.

**Second attempt.** Someone faces the thing as "made by some unknown maker." This does not face it as *God's* creation, so it confirms the statement rather than breaking it.

## 2. Repentance as return toward God and away from one's own turning

**Verdict: STANDS.**

**Attempted counterexamples:**
- **Repentance of a wrong done only to a neighbor.** This looks like repentance that is not away from turning from God's call or work. But a wrong against a neighbor is still a turning from His call, and it hinders His work for man. It falls inside the "or" clause.
- **"Repentance" by someone who never turned away.** Here there is nothing to return from. But this steps outside the statement's scope, because the statement defines repentance as presupposing one's own turning.
- **Turning from His call but not His work, or the reverse.** The disjunction "His call or His work" already covers both cases.

The statement is a definition with an inclusive disjunction. No case inside its terms makes it false.

## 3. A return is not fulfilled while a visible made thing is refused

**Verdict: STANDS.**

**First attempt: a creature one dislikes.** A person refuses to see a parasite as God's workmanship. One could argue this is not a turning from "His work" in statement 2's narrow sense, which is the immortality and eternal life of man. So the person's return might seem complete.

This fails because the refusal is willful. Refusing to acknowledge, in something one sees, that God made it is refusing God in His act of making. That is a remaining turning away from Him, so the return is not yet complete.

**Second attempt: legitimate refusal.**
- *Lot* is commanded not to look back at Sodom's destruction.
- *A person avoiding temptation* will not look at a human body presented for lust.

In both, the refusal seems right rather than a failure. But "face for what it is and as His workmanship" is not the same as "look at." One can obey by not looking and still acknowledge the destruction as God's doing, or the person as God's child. In fact, acknowledging the person that way is the right response, as opposed to lust.

**Third attempt: a fallen being.** A person shuns a fallen being that God made. Shunning it is not refusing to face it "for what it is," which now includes being fallen. Nor is it refusing to face it as originally His workmanship.

**Built-in limits.** The conditions "can see" and "refuses" exclude cases of ignorance and cases that are out of view. No case remains that satisfies all of the statement's terms while the return is fulfilled.

## Summary

All three statements stand. Every counterexample I found worked only by redefining "facing" as "looking," by adding the workmanship back into the first sense of "facing," or by stepping outside the scope of repentance.
