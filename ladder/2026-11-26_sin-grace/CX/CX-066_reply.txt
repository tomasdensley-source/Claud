**Verdict: BROKEN**

The statement is a biconditional: a past work rightly calls for a deed *exactly when* two conditions hold. So it fails if a case meets both conditions and yet the deed is not rightly called for. Such a case exists, because the second condition only weighs the new wrong's size and necessity. It never asks *whom* the wrong falls on or *what kind* of wrong it is.

**Counterexample**

Through my own reckless driving (my past work), I destroyed both of A's kidneys. A will die without a transplant. No donor is available except C, an unwilling stranger who had nothing to do with the crash. I can arrange to have one of C's kidneys removed by force. C would survive and live normally on one kidney.

Checking the statement's conditions:

1. **It answers an unanswered part of what the work did, to someone it touched.** A's loss of kidney function, and the death it will cause, has not been answered. A was touched by my work. The deed restores what my act took.
2. **It is proportional.** One functioning kidney is exactly what A needs to survive. That is neither more nor less than the harm requires.
3. **The new wrong is needed.** By stipulation, no other donor or means exists.
4. **The new wrong is less than the wrong it answers.** C forcibly loses one kidney but lives a normal life. A lost both kidneys and faces death. The new wrong is clearly smaller.

Every condition holds, so the statement says my past work rightly calls for this deed.

It does not. Forcibly taking an organ from an innocent third party is not something my wrongdoing calls for at all. It is a further serious violation, now aimed at someone my original act never touched. My debt to A is mine. It does not give me a claim on C's body. The past work calls for me to answer it with what is mine to give: my money, my effort, my confession, or my own kidney. It does not call for me to impose its cost on an uninvolved person, however much smaller that cost is.

**Why this stays within the statement's terms**

The case changes no word's meaning:
- "Those it touched" is satisfied by A.
- "Any new wrong" is satisfied by the harm to C.
- "Needed" and "less than" are satisfied by stipulation.

The case shows that "needed and lesser" is not enough to make a deed rightly called for. Constraints against using innocent third parties can make a deed impermissible even when it meets every condition in the formula. Since the formula claims those conditions are sufficient, the biconditional fails.
