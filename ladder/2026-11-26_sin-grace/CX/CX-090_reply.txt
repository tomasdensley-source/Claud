## Statement 1: **BROKEN**

The statement mostly gives definitions, and definitions cannot be falsified. But its final sentence makes a claim that is supposed to follow from them: "Whether sought or found, repentance *implies* turning toward God's creation as God's work, and toward oneself within it as a whole." The definitions given earlier do not secure that claim. A case can meet every stated term for repentance while the claimed result fails.

### Counterexample: selective repentance

1. A man lies to a friend and damages him. Later he understands what he did. For a while he stays turned away from it, which is sin by the statement's definition.
2. He then turns toward the freely given possibility of owning the lie. That gift is grace, as defined: "the free giving of that standing." Turning toward it is repentance, as defined.
3. He comes to stand toward the lie as his own and for what it is. He confesses it and makes the restitution it calls for. So his repentance now "contains sin's contrary," exactly as the statement requires.
4. At the same time, he clearly sees and understands a long-standing cruelty toward his brother. He deliberately keeps himself turned away from it.

Every definitional condition for repentance, and even for its completion in standing, is met for the lie. Yet he is not turned toward "oneself within it as a whole."

The statement's escape clause does not rescue it. "Though not every part of oneself can be seen" excuses only parts that cannot be seen. The cruelty here is seen and understood, and he refuses it on purpose.

Nothing in the definitions rules this out:
- Sin and its contrary are defined relative to a particular work ("that work").
- Grace is the giving of "that standing."
- Repentance is turning toward that grace.

Each of these is local to a work. Nothing in them forces the move to wholeness of self. The "implies" claims a result the terms do not produce.

### Supporting case: the God clause

An unbeliever goes through steps 1 to 3 in full. She receives the possibility of owning her deed as an unearned gift, perhaps through the forgiveness of the person she wronged. Grace, as defined, names no giver. So her repentance meets the definition while she does not turn toward creation *as God's work*.

The phrase "as God's work" parallels the earlier "as one's own." There, "as" clearly describes how the agent holds the thing. Read that way, the implication fails.

### The available defenses, and why they do not save the claim

**Defense 1: read the final sentence as an extra defining condition.** Then repentance would not count as repentance unless it turned toward the whole self and toward God's creation. This changes "implies" from a consequence into a stipulation. It also contradicts the earlier sentence, which says what repentance *is*: turning toward grace.

**Defense 2: read "turning toward oneself as a whole" as a bare direction, compatible with remaining sin.** This empties the phrase. It cannot cover someone who knowingly and deliberately excludes a part of himself that he can see.

**Defense 3: read "as God's work" as merely describing what creation in fact is.** This saves the God clause only by making it trivially true within the statement's framework. It does nothing for the selective-repentance case.

Read as written, the statement's own definitions allow a repentance that lacks the wholeness it claims repentance always implies. So the statement is broken.
