**1. BROKEN**

**Counterexample: the tax collector in Luke 18:9–14.**

The case is squarely in scope. It is repentance directed toward God, it answers his own sin, and the text gives the verdict that it succeeded: he "went down to his house justified." It cannot be dismissed as something other than repentance toward God as the answer to sin.

It fails two of the statement's requirements.

**(a) He is not "one who has remained turned away from what he has made or done."** He stands, beats his breast, and names himself by his deed: "a sinner." His sin is fully in front of him. His repentance is not a turning toward a deed he had been avoiding. It is a turning toward God from a deed he is already facing. David's "my sin is ever before me" (Psalm 51:3) follows the same pattern.

**(b) He does not consent to receive standing "and not only release."** He asks for one thing only: "God, be merciful to me." That is a petition for release. His posture explicitly declines standing:
- he "stood afar off,"
- he "would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven."

He asks for no restored standing toward his work and consents to none. Yet his repentance is accepted as complete.

**Anticipated defense: "justified" means God gave him standing anyway.** This does not rescue the statement. The statement places the consent to standing inside the penitent's act of repentance, as part of what repentance *is*. What God gives in response is a separate matter. Here the penitent's act consists only of facing his sin and asking for mercy, and that act is counted as repentance toward God. So the statement's definition does not hold as a necessary account.
