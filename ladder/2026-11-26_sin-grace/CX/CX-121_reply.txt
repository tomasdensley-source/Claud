**1. BROKEN**

The statement claims that repentance, the answer to sin, *is* a turning toward a free giving, by God or another, of standing toward one's work. That giving is part of what repentance is, not an optional accompaniment. So a case that fully answers the sin the statement describes, with no giver, breaks it.

**Counterexample: a solitary reckoning with no giver.**

A man once, in panic, abandoned a companion who died as a result. For twenty years he has remained turned away from that act. He will not think of it, retells it to himself as an accident, and avoids the place. This is exactly the condition the statement names: one who has remained turned away from what he has done.

His situation has three features:
- The companion is dead, so no forgiveness from the victim is possible.
- No one else knows, so no third party can offer him anything.
- Within the case there is no God, so there is no unnamed giver.

At fifty, alone, he reconstructs the night honestly and stops lying to himself: "I left him. That was me." He returns to the place. He lets the act stand as his, without excusing it and without collapsing into despair. He changes how he lives in light of it.

**Why this breaks the statement:**

- **It is within scope.** The case uses the statement's own account of sin, a remaining turned away from what one has done. Its ending is the direct reversal of that condition: he now stands turned toward the work. If anything counts as "the answer to sin" in the statement's terms, this does.
- **The required element is absent.** His turning is not toward any free giving. Nothing is given by God or by another, and he consents to receive nothing.
- **The grace clause does not rescue it.** "Whether or not he names it grace" covers a giving that goes unrecognized. It does not cover a case where no giving exists. To save the statement, one would have to insist there is always a giver. That is a metaphysical premise the statement does not state; it says "by God or another," not "God exists and always gives."
- **Rejecting the case requires an unstated premise.** The strongest defence is to say this is not repentance but mere self-reckoning, because standing toward one's work cannot be self-given. But that only works by adding a thesis the statement never states, namely that the turned-away person cannot turn back unaided. Without that thesis, the case satisfies every stated term while the claimed structure is missing.

**A second case from within the theological frame: the prodigal son.**

The son "came to himself" and turned homeward, which the tradition commonly reads as his repentance. At that point he explicitly refused the standing of son ("I am no longer worthy to be called your son") and sought only a servant's place. So he repented before, and against, consenting to receive the restored standing. That consent, if it came at all, came later in the father's embrace.

A defender could reply that his repentance was only completed by accepting the robe. That reply makes the second case contestable, which is why the first case carries the verdict.

**Verdict:** the statement fails as a necessary account of repentance.
