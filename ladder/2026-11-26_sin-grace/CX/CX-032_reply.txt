**1. BROKEN**

**Counterexample.** A driver's negligence killed a pedestrian years ago. The victim's family asked, through their lawyer, never to be contacted. The driver now decides to face what he did. He shows up unannounced at the family's home. He delivers a tearful public apology filmed for social media and presses a large cash payment on them. All of this is aimed squarely at confronting his own past act, and all of it is possible. None of it is fitting. It violates the family's explicit wishes, turns their grief into his spectacle, and reopens the wound it claims to address.

This satisfies every term of the statement:
- It is a deed.
- The past work it faces is his own.
- It is something that remains possible.

Yet it is not "fitting." So the deed by which a person faces their past work is not, as a matter of fact, limited to what is fitting. People routinely face their past deeds through gestures that are disproportionate, self-serving, intrusive or self-punishing. Examples include self-harm as penance, or a confession that burdens an innocent party.

**Why this is not a change of meaning.** The statement does not say "the proper deed" or "the deed one ought to do." It says "the deed in which a past work of one's own is faced *is limited to*" what is possible and fitting. That is a limiting claim about such deeds. The "possible" half holds trivially, since no one does the impossible. The "fitting" half is a norm presented as a boundary, and actual unfitting deeds of facing the past cross it.

**Caveat.** A defender could rescue the statement only by reading "is limited to" as "ought to be limited to," or by stipulating that an unfitting response does not count as genuinely "facing" one's work. Either move rewrites the statement into a normative or definitional claim it does not state.

**The second clause.** "Where nothing at all can be done, it *can* consist in willing acceptance" is only a claim that something is possible. One coherent case suffices to make it true, so it is not easily refuted. The verdict therefore rests on the first clause.
