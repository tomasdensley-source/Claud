# Verdicts on the Two Statements on Repentance

## 1. BROKEN

**The claim:** Repentance cannot be taking place unless the person faces God's creation as God's work.

**Counterexample 1 (inside a theological frame):** Take a second-century Marcionite Christian. He sincerely confesses his sins, grieves over them, turns from them, and seeks forgiveness from the God revealed in Christ. He is repenting toward God, in a Christian setting. But his theology explicitly denies that the material world is the work of that God. He attributes it to a lesser demiurge. So he does not face creation as God's work, and in fact rejects that view, yet repentance is taking place. The necessity claim fails.

**Counterexample 2 (ordinary sense of the word):** An atheist recognizes the harm she did to a friend. She feels genuine contrition, confesses, makes amends, and changes her conduct. That is repentance as the word is normally used, and the statement does not restrict "repentance" to a narrower meaning. It is achieved with no reference to creation or God at all.

**Limits of the defense:** Someone could reply that this "isn't real repentance." That reply only works by redefining repentance as "turning to God *as Creator*." Under that definition the statement becomes true by definition rather than a substantive claim. Without that redefinition, the statement is broken.

## 2. BROKEN

This statement makes three claims:
- facing creation (and oneself within it as a whole) is a *necessary implication* of repentance;
- this holds "whether it is sought or found";
- it is *not the objective* of repentance.

**Against the necessity claim:** The Marcionite counterexample still applies. Necessary implication means that wherever repentance occurs, this facing follows. The Marcionite repents and, by conviction, never faces creation as God's work. The same goes for the atheist. Nothing in the added wording ("oneself within it as a whole," "sought or found") limits the scope enough to exclude these cases.

**Against the "not its objective" claim:** Take a believer who sees that her sin was contempt for the created world. Examples would be cruelty to creatures, despising her own body, or treating people as raw material. Her repentance is explicitly aimed at learning again to see creation, and herself within it, as God's work. Here that facing is precisely what she seeks, so it is the objective of her repentance. The statement's own clause "whether it is sought" concedes that it can be sought. Once it is sought as the point of repenting, the claim that it is never the objective is false in her case.

**Limits of the defense:** This could be resisted only by reading the statement as a narrow normative claim, such as "it ought not to be *the essential* aim of repentance." That reading changes what the words say.
