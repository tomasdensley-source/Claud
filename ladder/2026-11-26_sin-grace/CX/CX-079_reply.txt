## 1. BROKEN

**Counterexample.** A person is brought, without having looked for it, to face what they have done, for instance a long habit of cruelty. They receive this facing as something given to them, not something they achieved, and they turn toward that gift. That meets every term of the definition: a turning toward the grace that freely gives the facing of one's own work.

At that moment, this person has not first faced creation as God's work. Their attention is wholly on their own deeds and on the grace that lets them see those deeds. Any recognition of creation as God's work comes later, as a result of the repentance. It was not a condition that had to be met before the repentance could occur.

Statement 1 says the facing of creation is "necessary for that repentance to be taking place." The natural reading of that phrase is that it must be in place for repentance to occur. On that reading, the statement turns a consequence into a precondition. The definition makes only one thing constitutive: turning toward the grace that gives the facing of one's own work. Requiring the facing of creation in advance would make repentance depend on something other than the grace it turns toward. That contradicts the "freely" in the definition.

**Caveat.** If "necessary" is read only as a logical necessary condition (whatever follows from X is necessary for X), then Statement 1 says nothing beyond Statement 2. In that case it would stand only by collapsing into Statement 2. As a distinct claim, it fails.

## 2. STANDS

**Attempted counterexample.** Someone faces their own deeds honestly but never faces creation, or themselves, as God's work. For example, they regard the world, and their own existence and abilities, as self-made or as chance.

**Why it fails.** That person is no longer inside the statement's terms. If they treat their facing of their own work as their own achievement, or as coming from no giver, they are not turning toward a grace that *freely gives* that facing. Once the terms are kept intact, the implication follows for three reasons:

- **One's own work has limits.** To face one's work as one's own is to face it against what one did not make. That includes one's own existence and the capacity to act at all.
- **A freely given gift points to a giver.** If the facing itself is received as freely given, it is received as someone else's work, namely God's.
- **Together these cover the whole.** One's own work therefore sits within what is not one's own work: creation and oneself within it, taken as a whole, as God's work.

**"Whether or not it is sought."** This also holds. The facing is given freely, not earned by seeking, so what it implies does not depend on seeking either.

I found no case that satisfies the definition while lacking this facing of creation.
