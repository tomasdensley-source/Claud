**1. BROKEN**

The statement is framed as a definition ("Repentance toward God *is*…"). So it fails if a case meets every stated condition and is still not repentance toward God, or if a case is repentance toward God and does not meet the conditions. The first kind of case can be built entirely within the statement's own terms.

**Counterexample (the conditions are met, but there is no repentance):**

A man abandons his wife and children. On any reading this is turning from God's work. Later, his father's will leaves the estate only to a son "living with his family." The man goes back to his household.

He also fully accepts the theology of the matter. He openly calls his wife and children God's gift and God's handiwork, and he means it as a factual belief. But he goes back only to secure the inheritance. Privately he resents God, has no sorrow for what he did, and does not submit his will to God. He is like the demons who "believe, and tremble" (James 2:19): he acknowledges God's work and remains opposed to God.

Each clause of the statement holds:
- **"One who has turned from… God's work":** he abandoned God's work in his family.
- **"The return toward what God has made":** he has actually gone back to them.
- **"Taken as God's work":** he sincerely holds that they are God's work.

Yet no ordinary or theological sense of "repentance toward God" covers this case. Nothing in him has turned toward God. There is no contrition and no change of allegiance.

**Why this is a valid counterexample, not a redefinition:**

The statement itself places the object of the return in "what God has made," not in God. It names no change of heart and no reorientation toward God as a person. Taking creation "as God's work" is a matter of how one regards it. It can be held with hostility, or with indifference, as well as with devotion.

A defender could argue that "return" already implies a heart turned toward God. That would add a condition the statement does not contain. Adding it would also concede that the stated formula is not enough to define repentance.

**Conclusion:**

The definition is too broad. It describes, at most, an outward effect that may accompany repentance toward God. It does not describe repentance itself. The statement cannot be assumed true as a definition.
