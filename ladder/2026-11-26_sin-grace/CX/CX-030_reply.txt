**1. BROKEN**

**The statement's claim.** It sets out a rule that depends on what is still possible. Where repair can be made, the deed of facing one's past work is repair. Where it cannot, the deed is amends or changed conduct. Where nothing can be done, it is willing acceptance. A valid counterexample is a case where repair can plainly be made, yet repair is not how the work is properly faced.

**Counterexample 1: repair is possible but should not be made.** Years ago, a man spread a cruel rumor that drove a former partner out of a town. He can still repair the damage. He can write to everyone he told and retract it. He can contact her, apologize, and offer to pay her relocation costs. All of this is concretely within his power.

But she has rebuilt her life. Most people have forgotten the rumor. Through a mutual friend, she has said she never wants to hear from him or have the matter raised again. Retracting the rumor would revive it among people who no longer remember it. Contacting her would violate her wishes and reopen the injury.

Here the fitting way to face what he did is not repair. It is restraint: leaving her alone, and changing how he speaks about others. The rule gives the wrong answer. It says "repair where repair can be made," and repair can be made, yet making it would compound the wrong.

One reply is that her refusal means repair "cannot be made." That changes what the words mean. The repairs remain fully performable. What changed is whether they ought to be done. The statement ties the deed to what is *possible*, not to what is advisable. It therefore cannot handle cases where possibility and fittingness come apart.

**Counterexample 2: repair is possible but not enough.** A drunk driver smashes a neighbor's fence. He pays for a full replacement, so the damage is completely repaired, and he goes on driving drunk. On the statement's rule, repair was possible and repair was made, so the work has been faced. Plainly it has not. The harm came from a pattern of conduct, so facing it requires changed conduct even though repair was fully available.

The statement treats repair and changed conduct as tiers, where one replaces the other when repair is out of reach. Here they are joint requirements. The tiered structure fails.

**Verdict.** Both cases stay within the statement's terms: a past work of one's own, with repair actually possible. In each, the deed the statement assigns is not the deed by which the work is faced. In the first, repair is wrong. In the second, repair is insufficient. The statement therefore cannot be assumed true.
