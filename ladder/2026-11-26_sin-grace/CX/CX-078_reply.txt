## Verdicts

**1. BROKEN**
**2. STANDS**

---

### Statement 1: BROKEN

**The conflict.** The statement defines the facing that is sin's contrary with a limit. It is facing one's work "as far as these can be seen." Facing is also more than attention ("attention alone never reaches that facing"). So facing is a full standing-before something "for what it is," and it can extend only as far as sight extends.

The last clause then says repentance, "whether sought or found," implies facing "oneself within it as a whole." Here the limit is gone. Facing a whole for what it is means facing its parts. Facing, by the statement's own definition, reaches only what can be seen.

**Counterexample.** A person's repentance has fully arrived. They stand facing every deed of theirs that can be seen. They acknowledge each one as their own, right or wrong, and do what each rightly calls for. By the statement's terms, this repentance contains sin's contrary completely.

But this person also has parts that cannot be seen: forgotten deeds, motives hidden even from themselves, and effects of their actions they cannot trace. These are still part of themselves. Facing cannot reach the unseen. So this person cannot face themselves as a whole.

The statement says repentance implies that whole-facing. Yet here is a case of genuine, arrived repentance, meeting every term of the statement, where the whole-facing is impossible. The implication fails. This uses only the statement's own definition of facing. The only escape would be to quietly treat "as a whole" as meaning "as a whole, as far as can be seen." That would change the words, and the clause as written contains no such limit.

---

### Statement 2: STANDS

The added clause, "though not every part of oneself can be seen," removes the conflict above. Facing oneself as a whole now explicitly allows for parts that cannot be seen. This matches the "as far as these can be seen" limit in the definition of sin's contrary. Facing a whole does not require seeing every part of it.

**Strongest remaining attempt.** Repentance that is sought but has not arrived is still repentance, since it is the turning toward grace. By the statement's terms, it does not yet contain sin's contrary, so it does not yet face one's own work. Yet it supposedly implies facing oneself as a whole. Is that a contradiction?

It is not, because the two facings have different objects:
- **Sin's contrary** concerns facing "the work of one's own hand," meaning one's own deeds.
- **The final clause** concerns facing God's creation "as God's work," and oneself within it. That is, oneself as God's work, a creature.

Someone can face themselves as a whole creature within God's creation without yet facing their particular deeds. In fact, turning toward grace, a free gift, already involves acknowledging creation as God's and oneself as dependent within it. So sought repentance can imply the creation-facing while not yet containing sin's contrary, and nothing collides.

I found no case that meets all of the statement's terms and makes its claims false.
