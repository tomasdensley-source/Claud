# Verdicts on the Three Statements

## 1. BROKEN

**The claim:** Rebelling against God is not necessarily sinning against God, as shown by Moses and Jonah.

**Counterexample:** The statement's own cases refute it. Scripture names both acts as rebellion and treats both as sin.

**Moses (Numbers 20):**
- God says: "Because you did not believe in me, to uphold me as holy… you shall not bring this assembly into the land" (20:12).
- God calls the act rebellion: "you rebelled against my command" (20:24; 27:14).
- Deuteronomy 32:51 adds: "you broke faith with me."
- The penalty is severe: Moses is barred from Canaan.
- This is the very act the statement cites. The text calls it rebellion and judges it as unbelief, faithlessness and failure to honor God as holy. That is sin by any biblical measure.

**Jonah:**
- His flight brings divine discipline: the storm and the fish.
- His prayer from the fish (Jonah 2) is a return to God after defying God's command.
- In chapter 4, God rebukes his continuing resentment.
- Nothing in the text presents his rebellion as sinless.

**The wider framework:**
- 1 Samuel 15:23: "rebellion is as the sin of divination."
- Lamentations 3:42: "we have transgressed and rebelled."
- 1 John 3:4 defines sin as lawlessness.

In this framework, rebellion against God means defying God's command, and that is a form of sin. The two examples offered as proof that rebellion can be sinless are both explicitly judged as sin.

## 2. STANDS

**The claim:** Repentance is turning toward God, toward God's grace.

**Why no valid counterexample holds:** In the biblical-theological sense the statement uses, this is the standard definition. The Hebrew *shuv* means to turn or return. The Greek *metanoia* is paired with turning to God, as in Acts 3:19 ("repent and turn back") and Acts 26:20 ("repent and turn to God").

I tried three candidate counterexamples. Each fails because it changes what "repentance" means:

- **Judas "repented himself" (Matthew 27:3, KJV):** He turned toward despair, not toward God. But the Greek here is *metamelomai*, which means regret, not *metanoia*. Using it changes the word's meaning.
- **God "repenting" (Genesis 6:6; Jonah 3:10, KJV):** This is *nacham*, God relenting. It is a different sense of the word, so it is equivocation.
- **Secular repentance toward another person:** This steps outside the theological scope the statement plainly works within.

The statement may be incomplete, since repentance also includes turning away from sin. But incompleteness does not falsify it.

## 3. BROKEN

**The claim:** The inverse of turning toward God's grace is rebellion.

**Counterexample:** Turning away from grace can happen without any rebellion against God's command.

**The elder brother (Luke 15:25–32):**
- He says, "I have never disobeyed your command."
- The father does not dispute this.
- Yet he refuses to enter the feast, which is the father's grace extended to his brother.
- He turns away from grace while being, by his own account and unchallenged, obedient.

**The Galatians (Galatians 5:4):**
- "You who would be justified by the law have fallen from grace."
- They fall away from grace by trying to be more law-observant, not by defying God.

**Drifting (Hebrews 2:1–3):**
- The text warns of "drifting away" and "neglecting" salvation.
- This is a passive movement away from grace through neglect, not active revolt.

**The reverse also fails, by the statement set's own example:** When Moses struck the rock, God called it rebellion (Numbers 20:24). Yet Moses was acting as God's agent, bringing out God's provision of water. That act was not a turning away from God's grace.

Rebellion and turning away from grace overlap, but they are not inverses. The true inverse of turning toward grace is turning away from it, and that can take forms other than rebellion: self-righteousness, legalism, neglect.
