**1. Verdict: STANDS**

The statement does not report facts about an independently fixed "sin," "repentance," or "grace." It stipulates what these terms mean and how they relate. So a valid counterexample would have to show that the scheme contradicts itself, or that a case meeting all its conditions yields a result it rules out. I tried the strongest candidates. Each one fails within the statement's terms or works only by importing outside meanings.

**Attempts and why they fail:**

1. **The original wrongful act.** At the moment someone first commits a wrong, there is no prior "work" to be turned away from. So the act itself seems not to count as sin, only the later refusal does. This conflicts only with the ordinary or traditional meaning of "sin." The statement deliberately places sin in *remaining* turned away *after knowledge*. Calling the initial act "sin" anyway swaps in an outside definition, which is not allowed.

2. **The defiant owner.** A murderer says, "I did it, it was murder, and I'd do it again." He seems to face his work "as his own and for what it is" and yet remains sinful. But the contrary requires facing "in acknowledgment *and in deed*." He acknowledges the act but does not answer it in deed. He even re-enacts the turning-away. So he does not meet the contrary's conditions, and the scheme classifies him correctly.

3. **Disowning a good work.** Someone knowingly refuses to own a good deed, for example through false modesty. The scheme counts this as sin, which sounds odd. Within its terms, though, this is a consequence, not a contradiction: refusing to stand before one's own work for what it is matches the definition. Calling it "not really sin" again relies on an outside standard.

4. **Attention without deed.** Someone scrutinizes their wrong obsessively but never acts on it. The statement anticipates this case explicitly ("which attention alone does not do"). It therefore confirms the scheme rather than breaking it.

5. **Repentance that never arrives.** Remorse that never reaches the facing does not contain the contrary. That is exactly what "only once it arrives at that facing" says.

6. **Given versus enacted.** If grace is the facing "insofar as it is given," it might seem it cannot also be the agent's own act of facing in deed. But "insofar as" picks out one aspect of the same facing. It does not claim the facing is given *rather than* enacted. No contradiction follows unless one assumes from outside the statement that receiving and doing exclude each other.

7. **Loss of knowledge.** Someone knew, then lost the memory through amnesia or dementia. Are they "remaining turned away"? Read naturally, they are no longer turned away *from* anything they know. So they fall outside the definition rather than refuting it.

**Conclusion:** Every case that seems to break the statement either fails one of its own conditions (deed, knowledge, "for what it is," "insofar as") or replaces its stipulated meanings with conventional ones. I could not construct a valid counterexample from inside its terms.
